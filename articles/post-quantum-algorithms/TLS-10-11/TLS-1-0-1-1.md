### 2.1. TLS 1.1

#### 2.1.1 The cryptographic scheme


![Parameters](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition1-parameters.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.1 (parameters).</span> A TLS 1.1 CBC cipher suite fixes a block cipher $E$ with block size $b$ and a MAC $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$ with tag length $t$, where
- $b \in \{8, 16\}$: DES, 3DES-EDE and IDEA have $b = 8$ [[RFC 4346, App. A.5]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#appendix-A.5), and AES has $b = 16$ [[RFC 3268]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3268);
- $H \in \{\mathrm{MD5}, \mathrm{SHA\text{-}1}\}$, so $t \in \{16, 20\}$, and the tag is the untruncated digest, $d = t$.

A header is a pair $\mathrm{HDR} = \mathit{type} \,\|\, \mathit{version} \in \mathbb{B}^3$ with $\mathit{version} = \langle \texttt{0x0302} \rangle_2$, and the header space is $\mathcal{H}_{1.1} = \mathbb{B} \times \{\langle \texttt{0x0302} \rangle_2\}$. The record space is $\mathcal{R}_{1.1} = \bigcup_{n \le 2^{14}} \mathbb{B}^n$ [[RFC 4346, §6.2.1]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.2.1).

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.2 (MAC input).</span> For a sequence number $\mathit{sqn} \in [0, 2^{64})$, a header and a record, the string authenticated with $R$ is

$$\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) = \langle \mathit{sqn} \rangle_8 \,\|\, \mathrm{HDR} \,\|\, \langle |R| \rangle_2 ,$$

and the tag is $\mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big)$ [[RFC 4346, §6.2.3.1]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.2.3.1). $\mathrm{AD}$ has length $h = 13$, and its length field is $|R|$, not the $|C|$ carried on the wire, so the receiver can form $\mathrm{AD}$ only after decrypting.

<span style="color:#87A878">Hypothesis 2.3 (idealised key generation, null compression).</span> $\mathsf{Gen}$ outputs $K = (K_e, K_a)$ uniform and independent, in place of their PRF derivation from the master secret [[RFC 4346, §5, §6.3]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.3), and the compression method is null, so the compressed fragment is the record itself; the handshake is outside the model.

![Encoding](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition4-encoding.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.4 (TLS 1.1 encoding).</span> For a record $R$ and a tag $T \in \mathbb{B}^t$, the admissible padding lengths are

$$\mathcal{P}(R) = \big\{ p \in [0, 255] : |R| + t + p + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod b \big\},$$

and for $p \in \mathcal{P}(R)$ the encoding is $\mathsf{Encode}_{1.1}(R, T; p) = R \,\|\, T \,\|\, \langle p \rangle^{p+1}$, the $\mathtt{GenericBlockCipher}$ structure of [[RFC 4346, §6.2.3.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.2.3.2). $\mathcal{P}(R)$ is non-empty since $b \le 16$. A *padding policy* $\pi$ assigns to each record length a distribution on $\mathcal{P}(R)$, and the sender draws $p \leftarrow \pi(|R|)$.

##### 2.1.1.1 Encryption

![IV Procedures](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition5-iv-procedures.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.5 (IV procedures).</span> [[RFC 4346, §6.2.3.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.2.3.2) gives two ways of producing the IV field $C_0$, the second in two variants, all wire-compatible:
- $\iota = 1$: draw $C_0 \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{B}^b$ and use it as the CBC mask;
- $\iota = 2\mathrm{a}$: draw $R' \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{B}^b$, prepend it to the plaintext and CBC-encrypt with the fixed mask $\mu = \langle 0 \rangle^b$, so $C_0 = E_{K_e}(R')$;
- $\iota = 2\mathrm{b}$: as $2\mathrm{a}$ with mask $\mu = \rho$, the CBC residue (last ciphertext block) of the previous record, so $C_0 = E_{K_e}(R' \oplus \rho)$.

The RFC also permits a third alternative, a fixed or weakly generated block masked by $\rho$; its $C_0$ is predictable from the previous record, Proposition 2.7 fails for it, and it is excluded throughout. "Cryptographically strong random" blocks are modelled as uniform. The sender's state is $\sigma = (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r, \rho)$ with $\sigma_0 = (0, 0, \rho_0)$ for an arbitrary, possibly key-dependent $\rho_0 \in \mathbb{B}^b$; $\rho$ is read only when $\iota = 2\mathrm{b}$.

![Encryption](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition6-encryption.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.6 (encryption).</span> $\mathsf{Enc}^{\iota}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, R; \sigma)$:

1. $T \leftarrow \mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big)$.
2. $p \leftarrow \pi(|R|)$ and $P \leftarrow \mathsf{Encode}_{1.1}(R, T; p)$, parsed as $P_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, P_n$.
3. If $\iota = 1$, $C_0 \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{B}^b$. Otherwise $R' \xleftarrow{\$} \mathbb{B}^b$ and $C_0 \leftarrow E_{K_e}(R' \oplus \mu)$ with $\mu$ as in Definition 2.5.
4. $C_i \leftarrow E_{K_e}(P_i \oplus C_{i-1})$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$.
5. $\mathit{sqn}_s \leftarrow \mathit{sqn}_s + 1$ and $\rho \leftarrow C_n$; return $C = C_0 \,\|\, C_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, C_n$.

<span style="color:#87A878">Proposition 2.7 (the IV procedures coincide).</span> For every key $K$, state $\sigma$, header and record, the ciphertexts output by $\mathsf{Enc}^{1}$, $\mathsf{Enc}^{2\mathrm{a}}$ and $\mathsf{Enc}^{2\mathrm{b}}$ are identically distributed. In each, $C_0$ is uniform on $\mathbb{B}^b$ and independent of $K$, of $\sigma$ and of every earlier ciphertext. Consequently $\rho$ influences no output and may be deleted from the state.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> For $\iota = 1$ this is the definition. For $\iota \in \{2\mathrm{a}, 2\mathrm{b}\}$, the mask $\mu$ is a function of $\sigma$ alone, and $R'$ is drawn uniformly and independently of it, so $R' \oplus \mu$ is uniform on $\mathbb{B}^b$ and independent of $\mu$, of $K$ and of all earlier ciphertexts. Since $E_{K_e}$ is a permutation of $\mathbb{B}^b$, $C_0 = E_{K_e}(R' \oplus \mu)$ is uniform with the same independence. Steps 1, 2 and 4 do not depend on $\iota$: $T$ and $P$ are computed without reference to the IV, and $(C_1, \dots, C_n)$ is the same deterministic function of $(K_e, C_0, P)$ in all three procedures. The joint distribution of $(C_0, \dots, C_n)$ is therefore the same. $\square$

From here on $\iota$ is suppressed and the state is $\sigma = (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r)$.

##### 2.1.1.2 Decryption

![Padding predicate and stripping](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition8-padding-check.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.8 (padding predicate and stripping).</span> For $P \in \mathbb{B}^{\ell_P}$ with $\ell_P \ge t + 1$, let $p = P[\ell_P - 1]$, read as an integer in $[0, 255]$, and let

$$\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}(P) \iff \ell_P \ge p + 1 + t \ \text{ and } \ P[\ell_P - 1 - i] = p \ \text{ for every } 0 \le i \le p,$$

the second conjunct being evaluated only if the first holds. Define $\mathrm{strip}(P) = (R, T)$ with $r = \ell_P - p - 1 - t$, and $\mathrm{strip}_0(P) = (R, T)$ with $r = \ell_P - t$, where in both cases $R = P[0 \mathinner{.\,.} r-1]$ and $T = P[r \mathinner{.\,.} r+t-1]$. $\Phi_{1.1}$ is the map that applies $\mathrm{strip}$ when $\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}(P)$ holds and $\mathrm{strip}_0$ otherwise.

$\mathrm{strip}_0$ reads the RFC's "assume a zero-length pad and then compute the MAC" [[RFC 4346, §6.2.3.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.2.3.2) as stripping nothing beyond the tag, which is the reading of [[LT, §2]](https://www.hit.bme.hu/~buttyan/courses/BMEVIHIM132/abib/04-TLS/Lucky13.pdf).

![Decryption](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition9-decryption.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.9 (decryption).</span> $\mathsf{Dec}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, C; \sigma)$:

1. If $|C| \bmod b \ne 0$, or $n := |C|/b - 1 < 1$, or $nb < t + 1$, or $|C| > 2^{14} + 2048$, return $\bot$.
2. $P_i \leftarrow D_{K_e}(C_i) \oplus C_{i-1}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$; set $P \leftarrow P_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, P_n$ and $\ell_P \leftarrow nb$.
3. $\mathit{ok} \leftarrow \Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}(P)$ and $(R, T) \leftarrow \Phi_{1.1}(P)$.
4. $v \leftarrow \big[\mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}_r, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big) = T\big]$, computed on both branches.
5. $\mathit{sqn}_r \leftarrow \mathit{sqn}_r + 1$; return $R$ if $\mathit{ok} \wedge v$, else $\bot$.

Step 1 depends on $|C|$ alone. It enforces the block-multiple rule [[RFC 4346, §7.2.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-7.2.2) and the fragment bound $|C| \le 2^{14} + 2048$ [[RFC 4346, §6.2.3]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-6.2.3), which guarantees $r < 2^{16}$, so that $\langle r \rangle_2$ in step 4 is defined. The padding check is mandatory ("the receiver MUST check this padding"), and $\bot$ is the single alert $\mathtt{bad\_record\_mac}$, which §7.2.2 prescribes for padding and MAC failures alike. Two provisions of that section are not modelled: the $\mathtt{decryption\_failed}$ alert it still permits with a MAY, and the $\mathtt{record\_overflow}$ check on a record that decrypts to more than $2^{14} + 1024$ bytes. Without the latter, $\mathsf{Dec}$ is typed into $\bigcup_{n < 2^{16}} \mathbb{B}^n \cup \{\bot\}$ rather than into $\mathcal{R}_{1.1} \cup \{\bot\}$. The conjunct $\mathit{ok}$ in step 5 is not redundant: without it, truncating the final padding block of an honest ciphertext whose record and tag fill whole blocks yields a plaintext that fails $\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}$ with probability about $1 - 2^{-8}$ for a pseudorandom tag, yet on which $\mathrm{strip}_0$ recovers exactly the honest record and tag.

![Correctness](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/proposition10-correctness.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Proposition 2.10 (correctness).</span> For every $\iota$, key, header, record $R \in \mathcal{R}_{1.1}$ and matched state $\mathit{sqn}_r = \mathit{sqn}_s$, $\mathsf{Dec}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, \mathsf{Enc}^{\iota}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, R; \sigma); \sigma) = R$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> $|C| = b + |R| + t + p + 1$ is a multiple of $b$ with $n \ge 1$ and $nb \ge t + 1$, and $|C| \le 16 + 2^{14} + 20 + 256 < 2^{14} + 2048$, so step 1 passes. Under every $\iota$ the ciphertext satisfies $C_i = E_{K_e}(P_i \oplus C_{i-1})$ for $i \ge 1$, so step 2 recovers $P = R \,\|\, T \,\|\, \langle p \rangle^{p+1}$. Its last byte is $p$, and $\ell_P = |R| + t + p + 1 \ge p + 1 + t$ with the last $p + 1$ bytes equal to $p$, so $\mathit{ok}$ holds and $\mathrm{strip}$ returns $r = |R|$ together with $R$ and $T$. The two MAC inputs agree because $\mathit{sqn}_r = \mathit{sqn}_s$ and $|R|$ is recovered exactly, so $v$ holds. $\square$

$\mathtt{bad\_record\_mac}$ is fatal [[RFC 4346, §7.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-7.2), so a single decryption failure ends the session in which it occurs.

##### 2.1.1.3 Leakage

![HMAC input length](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition11-hmac-length.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.11 (HMAC input length).</span> For a plaintext $P$ reaching step 3 of Definition 2.9, let $\lambda_{1.1}(P) = h + r$ with $r$ as determined by $\Phi_{1.1}$:

$$\lambda_{1.1}(P) = \begin{cases} h + \ell_P - t - 1 - p, & \text{if } \Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}(P), \\ h + \ell_P - t, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

It is undefined for a ciphertext rejected at step 1, where no MAC is computed. The TLS 1.1 leakage models are Models A and B of §1.1.5 with $\lambda$ replaced by $\lambda_{1.1}$ and $\mathsf{Dec}$ by Definition 2.9. Statements numbered 1.n are those of §1, on TLS 1.2 CBC mode; statements numbered 2.n are those of this section.

<span style="color:#87A878">Hypothesis 2.12 (on the receiver).</span> (i) *Constant-time padding comparison*: the evaluation of $\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}$ in step 3 runs in time independent of $P$, so that $\lambda_{1.1}$ is the only plaintext-dependent term of decryption time (Hypothesis 1.10). (ii) *Compliant underflow handling*: when the length test of $\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}$ fails, the receiver proceeds to $\mathrm{strip}_0$ rather than skipping MAC verification (Hypothesis 1.9).

Clause (ii) is a genuine restriction: a receiver that skips the MAC on underflow, as the implementation documented in [[LT, §6, p. 15]](https://www.hit.bme.hu/~buttyan/courses/BMEVIHIM132/abib/04-TLS/Lucky13.pdf) does, falls outside the results below, on either version.


#### 2.1.2 TLS 1.1 is cryptographically equivalent to TLS 1.2 CBC mode

TLS 1.1 *is* TLS 1.2 CBC mode instantiated with a MAC whose inputs have one byte relabelled, so every adversary achieves the same advantage against both (Theorem 2.24). If HMAC is a PRF, TLS 1.1 and TLS 1.2 CBC mode with the standard MAC are equivalent up to a small additive term (Corollary 2.25).

![TLS 1.2 CBC mode](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition13-tls12-cbc.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.13 (TLS 1.2 CBC mode, RFC 5246 reading).</span> For a block cipher $E$, a keyed function family $F : \mathcal{K}_a \times \mathbb{B}^* \to \mathbb{B}^t$ and a padding policy $\pi$ as in Definition 2.4, $\Pi_{1.2}[E, F, \pi]$ is the scheme of Definitions 1.7 and 1.8 with $\mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}$ replaced by $F_{K_a}$, with the number of extra padding blocks drawn as $j = (p - p_{\min}(R))/b$ for $p \leftarrow \pi(|R|)$ (an integer by Lemma 2.19), with keys generated as in Hypothesis 2.18 and state $\sigma = (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r)$, $\sigma_0 = (0, 0)$, and with four corrections that bring it in line with [[RFC 5246]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246):
- (C0) the header space $\mathcal{H}$ and the record space $\mathcal{R} \subseteq \mathbb{B}^*$ of Definition 1.2 are parameters of the scheme rather than fixed at $\mathbb{B}^5$ and $\mathbb{B}^*$, its 5-byte header and $h = 13$ being recovered as $\mathrm{AD}$ of Definition 2.2;
- (C1) the header space is $\mathcal{H}_{1.2} = \mathbb{B} \times \{\langle \texttt{0x0303} \rangle_2\}$, the record space is $\mathcal{R}_{1.1}$ of Definition 2.1, with the same typing caveat as Definition 2.9, and both the sender (step 1 of Definition 1.7) and the receiver (step 7 of Definition 1.8) authenticate $\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R$ of Definition 2.2, the receiver computing the length field as $\langle r \rangle_2$ after depadding [[RFC 5246, §6.2.1, §6.2.3.1]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246#section-6.2.3.1);
- (C2) step 1 of Definition 1.8 also rejects $|C| > 2^{14} + 2048$ [[RFC 5246, §6.2.3]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246#section-6.2.3);
- (C3) step 7 of Definition 1.8 returns $R$ if $\mathit{wf} \wedge v$, and $\bot$ otherwise [[RFC 5246, §6.2.3.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246#section-6.2.3.2).

The TLS 1.2 scheme with its standard MAC is $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H, \pi]$.

**Remark (on the corrections).** (C0), (C1) and (C2) are bookkeeping: a fixed 5-byte header handed to the receiver cannot contain $|R|$ before depadding, and without a bound on $|C|$ the length field $\langle r \rangle_2$ is undefined. (C3) is substantive. Definition 1.8, as written, returns $R$ whenever the MAC verifies, even when $\mathit{wf}$ fails, so the truncation described after Definition 2.9, applied to a challenge with $|M_0| = |M_1| \ge 1$, $|M_0| + t \equiv 0 \pmod b$ and $j = 0$, wins the distinguishing game of Definition 1.17 with verdict-only advantage about $1 - 2^{-8}$ for a pseudorandom tag, contradicting the LH-AE claim of Proposition 1.27. Definition 1.8 should be amended by (C3).

![Translations](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition14-translations.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.14 (the translations).</span> The *header translation* is the bijection $\psi : \mathcal{H}_{1.1} \to \mathcal{H}_{1.2}$, $\psi(\mathit{type} \,\|\, \langle \texttt{0x0302} \rangle_2) = \mathit{type} \,\|\, \langle \texttt{0x0303} \rangle_2$. Let $\tau$ be the transposition of $\texttt{0x02}$ and $\texttt{0x03}$ on $\mathbb{B}$. The *input relabelling* is the map $\phi : \mathbb{B}^* \to \mathbb{B}^*$ that fixes every $x$ with $|x| \le 10$ and otherwise replaces the byte $x[10]$ by $\tau(x[10])$, leaving all other bytes unchanged. For a keyed family $F$, write $F \circ \phi$ for the family $(K, x) \mapsto F_K(\phi(x))$.

$\phi$ is a length-preserving involution, hence a permutation of each $\mathbb{B}^n$.

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.15 (the MAC inputs correspond).</span> For every $\mathit{sqn}$, every $\mathrm{HDR} \in \mathcal{H}_{1.1}$ and every $R \in \mathbb{B}^*$ with $|R| < 2^{16}$,

$$\phi\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \psi(\mathrm{HDR}), R) \,\|\, R\big) = \mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R ,$$

and consequently $(\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi)_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \psi(\mathrm{HDR}), R) \,\|\, R\big) = \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big)$ for every key.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> In $\mathrm{AD}$ the bytes $0$–$7$ hold $\langle \mathit{sqn} \rangle_8$, byte $8$ holds $\mathit{type}$, bytes $9$–$10$ hold the version and bytes $11$–$12$ hold $\langle |R| \rangle_2$. The two strings $\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \psi(\mathrm{HDR}), R) \,\|\, R$ and $\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R$ have length at least 13 and differ only at byte $10$, which is $\texttt{0x03}$ in the first and $\texttt{0x02}$ in the second. $\phi$ applies $\tau$ to exactly that byte. $\square$

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.16 (equivalence).</span> Let $\Pi$ and $\Pi'$ be schemes in the syntax of Definition 1.2, with header spaces $\mathcal{H}$ and $\mathcal{H}'$ and a bijection $\psi : \mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}'$. For an adversary $\mathcal{A}$ against $\Pi$, $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$ is the adversary against $\Pi'$ that runs $\mathcal{A}$, replaces every header $h$ that $\mathcal{A}$ submits by $\psi(h)$ and every header $h'$ that $\mathcal{A}$ receives by $\psi^{-1}(h')$, and passes records, ciphertexts, verdicts and leakage values unchanged. It makes exactly the queries of $\mathcal{A}$, and runs in the time of $\mathcal{A}$ plus one evaluation of $\psi$ or $\psi^{-1}$ per header. Let $\mathcal{G}$ be the set of games $\mathrm{PR}\text{-}X$ and $\mathrm{IND}\text{-}X$ for $X \in \{\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}\}$, as fixed by Definitions 1.15–1.17 and Models A and B of §1.1.5, played with the same parameters (number of sessions $q$, record index $k$, record distribution $\mathcal{M}$, target block index $m$, padding policy, leakage constants $c_0$ and $\sigma_{\!\eta}$) on both sides. $\Pi$ and $\Pi'$ are *exactly equivalent under $\psi$* if for every $G \in \mathcal{G}$, every $\mathcal{A}$ and every $q$,

$$\mathbf{Adv}^{G}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{A}, q) = \mathbf{Adv}^{G}_{\Pi'}(\mathcal{A}^{\psi}, q),$$

and *$\epsilon$-equivalent under $\psi$* if the two sides differ by at most $\epsilon(\mathcal{A}, q)$.

Both notions are symmetric: for an adversary $\mathcal{A}'$ against $\Pi'$, the adversary $(\mathcal{A}'^{\psi^{-1}})^{\psi}$ submits and receives exactly what $\mathcal{A}'$ does, so applying the definition to $\mathcal{A}'^{\psi^{-1}}$ gives $\mathbf{Adv}^{G}_{\Pi'}(\mathcal{A}', q) = \mathbf{Adv}^{G}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{A}'^{\psi^{-1}}, q)$, with the same slack in the approximate case whenever $\epsilon$ depends only on the query count and the running time, as that of Corollary 2.25 does. Equivalence thus transfers attacks in both directions. The advantage is written without the subscript $\mathcal{M}$ of Definition 1.16, $\mathcal{M}$ being held equal on both sides.

##### 2.1.2.1 The common parameter set

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.17 (common parameter set).</span> $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of pairs $(E, H)$ with $E$ a block cipher of block size $b \in \{8, 16\}$ and $H \in \{\mathrm{MD5}, \mathrm{SHA\text{-}1}\}$. This is the parameter set of Definition 2.1, and it lies inside that of Definitions 1.3–1.4, which admits $H \in \{\mathrm{MD5}, \mathrm{SHA\text{-}1}, \mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}\}$.

The HMAC-SHA-256 suites of TLS 1.2 ($t = 32$) have no TLS 1.1 counterpart, and nothing below applies to them. On $\mathcal{S}$ both schemes have the same $b$, $t$ and $h = 13$, and $\mathcal{S}$ contains the instantiation of §1, $b = 16$, $t = 20$ (AES with HMAC-SHA-1). The statements are made per algorithm pair, independently of which suites each RFC registers.

##### 2.1.2.2 Scope: the record layer, with idealised key generation

<span style="color:#87A878">Hypothesis 2.18 (idealised key generation for TLS 1.2).</span> Hypothesis 2.3 holds for $\Pi_{1.2}$ as well: for each $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$, $\mathsf{Gen}$ outputs $K = (K_e, K_a)$ uniform and independent, of the key lengths fixed by $(E, H)$, which are the same in RFC 4346 and RFC 5246. Compression is null.

The versions derive these keys through different PRFs, MD5 $\oplus$ SHA-1 in [[RFC 4346, §5]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4346#section-5) and $P_{\mathrm{SHA256}}$ in [[RFC 5246, §5]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5246#section-5), and their handshakes differ; Hypotheses 2.3 and 2.18 set this aside, so the equivalence concerns the record layer only.

##### 2.1.2.3 Encoding

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.19 (encodings coincide).</span> For every $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$, every record $R$ and every tag $T \in \mathbb{B}^t$,

$$\mathcal{P}(R) = \big\{ p_{\min}(R) + jb : j \in \mathbb{N},\ p_{\min}(R) + jb \le 255 \big\},$$

and for $p \in \mathcal{P}(R)$, $\mathsf{Encode}_{1.1}(R, T; p) = \mathsf{Encode}(R, T; j)$ of Definition 1.5 with $j = (p - p_{\min}(R))/b$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> If $p = p_{\min}(R) + jb \le 255$, then $p \ge 0$ and $|R| + t + p + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod b$ by Proposition 1.6, so $p \in \mathcal{P}(R)$. Conversely, if $p \in \mathcal{P}(R)$ then $|R| + t + p + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod b$ and $p \ge 0$, and the converse part of Proposition 1.6 gives $p = p_{\min}(R) + jb$ for some $j \in \mathbb{N}$. Both encodings are $R \,\|\, T \,\|\, \langle p \rangle^{p+1}$. $\square$

A padding policy $\pi$ on $\mathcal{P}(R)$ thus corresponds bijectively to a distribution on $j$. A deterministic policy $\pi_j$, $p = p_{\min}(R) + jb$, corresponds to the fixed $j$ of Definitions 1.7 and 1.15.

##### 2.1.2.4 Encryption

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.20 (encryption coincides).</span> Fix $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$ and a padding policy $\pi$. For every key $K$, state $\sigma = (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r)$, header $\mathrm{HDR} \in \mathcal{H}_{1.1}$, record $R \in \mathcal{R}_{1.1}$ and IV procedure $\iota$, the output $(C, \sigma')$ of $\mathsf{Enc}^{\iota}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, R; \sigma)$ in $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$ has the same distribution as the output of $\mathsf{Enc}_K(\psi(\mathrm{HDR}), R; \sigma)$ in $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$. The same holds conditionally on any history of earlier calls. More strongly, for $\iota = 1$ the two outputs are equal when both calls use the same $C_0$ and the same $p$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> By Proposition 2.7 it suffices to treat $\iota = 1$. Couple the two calls on the same $p \leftarrow \pi(|R|)$ and the same $C_0$, which in both schemes is fresh and uniform given $K$, $\sigma$ and every earlier ciphertext (by Definition 2.6, and by step 3 of Definition 1.7); this is what makes the statement hold conditionally on history. In step 1, the tags agree by Lemma 2.15, since $|R| \le 2^{14}$. In step 2, the plaintexts agree by Lemma 2.19. In step 4, $C_i = E_{K_e}(P_i \oplus C_{i-1})$ in both. In step 5, both increment $\mathit{sqn}_s$ and nothing else in the state is read afterwards (Proposition 2.7). The outputs are therefore equal under this coupling, and since the coupled coins are identically distributed in the two schemes, the output distributions coincide. $\square$

##### 2.1.2.5 Decryption

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.21 (decryption coincides).</span> Fix $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$. For every key $K$, state $\sigma$, header $\mathrm{HDR} \in \mathcal{H}_{1.1}$ and $C \in \mathbb{B}^*$, $\mathsf{Dec}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, C; \sigma)$ of Definition 2.9 and $\mathsf{Dec}_K(\psi(\mathrm{HDR}), C; \sigma)$ in $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$ return the same output and the same updated state. When $C$ passes step 1, the two computations moreover produce the same $P$, the same $p$, the same value $\mathit{ok} = \mathit{wf}$ and the same $r$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Both algorithms are deterministic; we compare them step by step.
- *Length test.* Step 1 of Definition 2.9 and step 1 of Definition 1.8 with (C2) test the same four conditions, and both return $\bot$ without touching the state.
- *CBC decryption.* Step 2 is identical in both.
- *Padding check.* $p$ is the last byte of $P$ in both. $\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}(P)$ of Definition 2.8 is, verbatim, the predicate $\mathit{wf}$ of step 4 of Definition 1.8, with the same length test and the same order of evaluation.
- *Stripping.* $\Phi_{1.1}$ applies $\mathrm{strip}$, $r = \ell_P - p - 1 - t$, exactly when $\mathit{wf}$ holds, and $\mathrm{strip}_0$, $r = \ell_P - t$, otherwise; these are the two branches of step 5 of Definition 1.8. $R$ and $T$ are then cut identically, as in its step 6.
- *MAC.* Step 1 gives $r \le \ell_P - t < 2^{14} + 2048 < 2^{16}$, so the length field is defined in both. By (C1) and Lemma 2.15, $(\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi)_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}_r, \psi(\mathrm{HDR}), R) \,\|\, R\big) = \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}_r, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big)$, so $v$ is the same bit.
- *Output.* Step 5 of Definition 2.9 returns $R$ iff $\mathit{ok} \wedge v$, which is (C3). Both increment $\mathit{sqn}_r$ exactly once, after computing $v$. $\square$

##### 2.1.2.6 Leakage

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.22 (leakage coincides).</span> Fix $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$. For every $C$ passing step 1, with $P$ its decrypted plaintext, $\lambda_{1.1}(P)$ of Definition 2.11 equals $\lambda(P)$ of Definition 1.11 computed in $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$. Consequently the Model A and Model B oracles of the two schemes return the same second component on the same query, given the same noise $\eta$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Both equal $h + r$ with $h = 13$, and $r$ is the same by Lemma 2.21. In Model A the second component is $\mathsf{cost}_H(\lambda)$. $\mathsf{cost}_H$ counts the compression-function evaluations of $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$ on an input of the given length, and $\phi$ preserves length, so $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi$ performs exactly the same count, $\mathsf{cost}_{H}(\ell)$, on every input of length $\ell$. Proposition 1.13 applies to both $H \in \{\mathrm{MD5}, \mathrm{SHA\text{-}1}\}$: each has a 64-byte block, an 8-byte length encoding and $d \le 55$, and $|K_a| = d \le 64$. For a ciphertext rejected at step 1, both oracles return $\varnothing$. In Model B the second component is $c_0 \cdot \mathsf{cost}_H(\lambda) + \eta$ with the same $c_0$, $\sigma_{\!\eta}$ and $\eta$. $\square$

Fixing $c_0$ and $\sigma_{\!\eta}$ equal on both sides, as Definition 2.16 does, models one implementation that speaks both versions.

##### 2.1.2.7 Session model and games

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.23 (the games are the same games).</span> For every $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$ and every padding policy, Definitions 1.15, 1.16 and 1.17 are well defined for $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$ with the same parameters as for $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$. For $\mathcal{M}$, $j$ and $m$ fixed, the target block $P^\ast$ is the same function of $R^\ast$ in both.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Those definitions use the scheme only through four things:
- $\mathsf{Gen}$, $\mathsf{Enc}$, $\mathsf{Dec}$ and the leakage oracle, which for $\Pi_{1.1}$ are given by Hypothesis 2.3, Definitions 2.6 and 2.9 and Definition 2.11;
- the sequence numbers, which in both schemes start at $0$ and are incremented once per encryption and once per decryption reaching the MAC;
- the padding parameter $j$ of Definition 1.15, which for $\Pi_{1.1}$ is the deterministic policy $\pi_j$ of §2.1.2.3;
- the block structure of the encoding, which is identical by Lemma 2.19.

Definition 1.16 admits an index $m$ exactly when the $m$-th block of the encoding lies within $R^\ast$. That depends only on $|R^\ast|$, $t$, $b$ and $p$, which agree on both sides, so the same $m$ are admissible and $P^\ast = R^\ast[(m-1)b \mathinner{.\,.} mb - 1]$ in both. The one-query-per-session rule is imposed by the experiment itself. $\square$

##### 2.1.2.8 The equivalence theorem

![Exact equivalence](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/theorem24-equivalence.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Theorem 2.24 (exact equivalence).</span> For every $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$ and every padding policy $\pi$, $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$ and $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$ are exactly equivalent under $\psi$ in every game of $\mathcal{G}$, the policy being deterministic, $\pi = \pi_j$, in the plaintext-recovery games as Definition 1.15 requires.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Fix $G \in \mathcal{G}$, an adversary $\mathcal{A}$ and $q$. World 1 is $G$ played by $\mathcal{A}$ against $\Pi_{1.1}$. World 2 is $G$ played by $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$ against $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$.

*Step 1 (IV procedure).* Replace $\iota$ by $1$ in every encryption of world 1. By Proposition 2.7, each encryption's output is distributed identically under every $\iota$, conditionally on the keys, the state and all earlier ciphertexts. Every input $\mathcal{A}$ supplies is a function of its coins and of earlier oracle answers. An induction on the number of oracle calls therefore shows that the joint distribution of $\mathcal{A}$'s view, together with $R^\ast$ and $d$, is unchanged.

*Step 2 (coupling).* Run the two worlds on shared randomness, which is identically distributed on both sides:
- the keys of each session, by Hypotheses 2.3 and 2.18;
- $R^\ast$ and, in IND, the bit $d$;
- for each encryption call, its $C_0$ and $p$;
- for each Model B query, its noise $\eta$;
- the coins of $\mathcal{A}$;
- the records encrypted at the indices before $k$ in each session, which Definition 1.15 leaves unspecified and which the coupling therefore takes to be generated by the same rule on both sides.

We show by induction on the interaction that after each oracle call the two worlds hold the same states and every message $\mathcal{A}$ receives in world 1 equals the corresponding message $\mathcal{A}$ receives inside $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$ in world 2, after $\psi^{-1}$ has been applied to headers. Initially the states are $(0, 0)$ in every session of both worlds. The experiment's own encryptions (the challenge ciphertexts) are equal by Lemma 2.20 under the coupling. The next query of $\mathcal{A}$ is a function of its view and coins, so it is equal in both worlds, and $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$ forwards it with $\psi$ applied to its header. No game of $\mathcal{G}$ gives the adversary an encryption oracle, so every query is a decryption query; its verdict and new state are equal by Lemma 2.21, and its leakage component by Lemma 2.22, which applies because both worlds decrypt the same $P$ with the same $\eta$. Because the challenge ciphertexts are equal and $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$ passes records and ciphertexts through unchanged, a query violates a restriction in world 2 exactly when it does in world 1: the one query per session and the ban on the session's own challenge ciphertext in Definition 1.15, and $|M_0| = |M_1|$ with one non-challenge decryption query in Definition 1.17.

*Step 3 (winning).* At the end, $\mathcal{A}$ outputs the same $\hat{P}$ or $\hat{d}$ in both worlds. The winning condition compares it with $P^\ast$, the same function of $R^\ast$ by Lemma 2.23, or with $d$, which is shared. The winning events therefore coincide under the coupling, the winning probabilities are equal, and so are the advantages, which are the same function of the winning probability in both worlds. The converse direction is the symmetry noted after Definition 2.16. $\square$

![Hybrids](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/corollary25-hybrids.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Corollary 2.25 (equivalence with the standard MAC).</span> Assume $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$ is a pseudorandom function on inputs of length at most $L = 13 + 2^{14} + 2048$. Let $\epsilon$ be the largest PRF advantage $\mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{prf}}_{\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H}$ of an adversary that makes as many queries as there are MAC evaluations in one session of $G$, and runs in about the time of $G$. Then for every $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$, every $X \in \{\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B}\}$, every $\mathcal{A}$, every $q$ and every $\pi$ admissible for the game as in Theorem 2.24:

$$\Big| \mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{PR}\text{-}X}_{\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]}(\mathcal{A}, q) - \mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{PR}\text{-}X}_{\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H, \pi]}(\mathcal{A}^{\psi}, q) \Big| \le 2q\,\epsilon ,$$

$$\Big| \mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND}\text{-}X}_{\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]}(\mathcal{A}) - \mathbf{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND}\text{-}X}_{\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H, \pi]}(\mathcal{A}^{\psi}) \Big| \le 4\epsilon .$$

That is, TLS 1.1 and TLS 1.2 CBC mode are $2q\epsilon$-equivalent under $\psi$ in the plaintext-recovery games, and $4\epsilon$-equivalent in the distinguishing games.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> By Theorem 2.24 it suffices to compare $\Pi_{1.2}[E, F, \pi]$ for $F = \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi$ and for $F = \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$, both against $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$. Let $D = \bigcup_{n \le L} \mathbb{B}^n$, which contains every MAC input either scheme ever forms. Consider four hybrids, each session keyed independently:
- $\Gamma_0$: the game with $F = \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi$;
- $\Gamma_1$: the game with $F = \rho \circ \phi$, where each session gets its own uniformly random function $\rho : D \to \mathbb{B}^t$;
- $\Gamma_2$: the game with $F = \rho$;
- $\Gamma_3$: the game with $F = \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$.

$\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ are identical, since $\phi$ permutes $D$ and so $\rho \circ \phi$ is again a uniformly random function on $D$.

For $\Gamma_0 \to \Gamma_1$, use a hybrid over the $q$ sessions. In the $i$-th step, sessions before $i$ use $\rho \circ \phi$, sessions after $i$ use $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi$ under a key it samples, and session $i$ uses the challenge oracle composed with $\phi$. A PRF adversary $\mathcal{B}_i$ simulates this step:
- it samples every $K_e$, every other session's MAC key or random function (lazily), all encryption coins and all noise;
- it answers session $i$'s MAC evaluations through its oracle, applying $\phi$ to each input first;
- it computes each leakage value $\mathsf{cost}_H(\lambda(P))$ itself, which is possible because the leakage is a function of $P$ and $H$ and not of $K_a$;
- it outputs 1 iff $\mathcal{A}^{\psi}$ wins.

Each step changes the winning probability by at most $\epsilon$, so $|\Pr_{\Gamma_0}[\mathrm{win}] - \Pr_{\Gamma_1}[\mathrm{win}]| \le q\epsilon$. The same argument without $\phi$ gives $|\Pr_{\Gamma_2}[\mathrm{win}] - \Pr_{\Gamma_3}[\mathrm{win}]| \le q\epsilon$. The PR advantage is the winning probability, which gives the first bound. The IND game has $q = 1$, and its advantage $|2\Pr[\mathrm{win}] - 1|$ changes by at most twice the change in winning probability, which gives $2 \cdot 2\epsilon$. $\square$

**Remark (what the equivalence says).** TLS 1.1 is TLS 1.2 CBC mode instantiated with $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi$, a MAC with the same key, the same tag length, the same compression-function count on every input, and the same security as $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$ under every standard notion, since $\phi$ is an efficiently computable, length-preserving permutation. Hence any statement about $\Pi_{1.2}[E, F, \pi]$ that holds for every $F$ with the cost function of $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$ holds for TLS 1.1 verbatim, with no loss. This covers any attack whose analysis uses only the leakage and the padding structure, never the values of the MAC, and is the route taken in §2.1.3. Corollary 2.25 is needed only for statements specific to $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H$ itself, and rests on the PRF assumption, which is stronger than the SUF-CMA assumption of Definition 1.4.


#### 2.1.3 Attacks against TLS 1.2 CBC mode also break TLS 1.1

![Attack transfer](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/corollary26-attack-transfer.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Corollary 2.26 (TLS 1.1 offers no confidentiality either).</span> Let $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$ with $b = 16$ and $t = 20$, let $\pi$ be deterministic and let the receiver satisfy Hypothesis 2.12. Under the multi-session interface of Definition 1.15, with $k \ge 1$, $|R^\ast| \ge b$ and the challenge ciphertext excluded from queries, the adversary $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{\psi^{-1}}$ recovers in Model A any target block lying wholly within the challenge record of $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$ with advantage exactly $1$, under no cryptographic assumption, from at most $M = 2^{16} + 43 + 14 \cdot 2^{8}$ sessions of one decryption query each; in Model B the same recovery succeeds with probability at least $1 - \delta$ at $L \ge (8 \sigma_{\!\eta}^2/c_0^2) \ln(2M/\delta)$ repetitions per mask, $\delta$ being written $\epsilon$ in Theorem 1.26 and unrelated to the $\epsilon$ of Corollary 2.25.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Theorems 1.25 and 1.26 exhibit $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}$ with these advantages against the scheme of Definitions 1.7 and 1.8, read as written. Two transfers are needed before Theorem 2.24 applies. First, to the corrected scheme $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{MAC}, \pi]$ of Definition 2.13: (C0) and (C2) do not affect any query of $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}$, whose ciphertexts are four non-IV blocks; (C1) replaces the 5-byte header by $\mathrm{AD}$, which is again 13 bytes, so $h$, $\lambda$ and $\mathsf{cost}(\lambda)$ are untouched; and (C3) changes only the verdict component, on plaintexts where the padding check fails but the MAC verifies. By the remark on verdict-independence in §1.3, $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}$ decides on the count alone, which is fixed before the verdict, so its advantage is unchanged. Second, to $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$: the same proofs use the decryption oracle only through $\mathsf{cost}(\lambda(P))$ and the padding structure of the encoding, never through the value of a tag, and both are unchanged under the relabelling by Lemma 2.22 and the remark closing §2.1.2.8. So $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}$ attains the same advantages against $\Pi_{1.2}[E, \mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi, \pi]$ with the same session and query counts. Theorem 2.24, in the direction given by the symmetry of Definition 2.16, converts it into $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{\psi^{-1}}$ against $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$ with the advantage unchanged and one header relabelling per query added to its running time. $\square$

Every quantity of the Lucky Thirteen recovery (sessions, queries per session, advantage, noise scaling) thus carries over to TLS 1.1 exactly, and upgrading from TLS 1.1 to TLS 1.2 CBC mode is no defence against it.


### 2.2. TLS 1.0

#### 2.2.1 The cryptographic scheme

![TLS 1.0 parameters](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition27-parameters.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.27 (parameters).</span> A TLS 1.0 CBC cipher suite fixes the same pairs $(E, H)$ as Definition 2.1 [[RFC 2246, App. A.5]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#appendix-A.5), AES being added by [[RFC 3268]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3268). The header space is $\mathcal{H}_{1.0} = \mathbb{B} \times \{\langle \texttt{0x0301} \rangle_2\}$ and the record space is $\mathcal{R}_{1.1}$ of Definition 2.1 [[RFC 2246, §6.2.1]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#section-6.2.1).

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.28 (MAC input).</span> The tag is $\mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big)$ with $\mathrm{AD}$ of Definition 2.2 read with the TLS 1.0 header [[RFC 2246, §6.2.3.1]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#section-6.2.3.1), so $h = 13$ here too.

<span style="color:#87A878">Hypothesis 2.29 (idealised key generation, null compression).</span> $\mathsf{Gen}$ outputs $K = (K_e, K_a)$ and the initial chaining value $\nu_0 \in \mathbb{B}^b$, all uniform and independent, in place of the key block and write IV of [[RFC 2246, §6.3]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#section-6.3); compression is null and the handshake is outside the model.

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.30 (encoding).</span> The encoding is $\mathsf{Encode}_{1.1}$ of Definition 2.4, with the same admissible set $\mathcal{P}(R)$ and padding policy $\pi$: [[RFC 2246, §6.2.3.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#section-6.2.3.2) specifies the same structure, padding rule and bound of 255.

##### 2.2.1.1 Encryption

![Implicit IV encryption](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition31-encryption.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.31 (encryption, implicit IV).</span> The state is $\sigma = (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r, \nu_s, \nu_r)$ with $\sigma_0 = (0, 0, \nu_0, \nu_0)$. $\mathsf{Enc}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, R; \sigma)$:

1. $T \leftarrow \mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big)$.
2. $p \leftarrow \pi(|R|)$ and $P \leftarrow \mathsf{Encode}_{1.1}(R, T; p)$, parsed as $P_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, P_n$.
3. $C_0 \leftarrow \nu_s$, the last ciphertext block of the previous record, or $\nu_0$ for the first.
4. $C_i \leftarrow E_{K_e}(P_i \oplus C_{i-1})$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$.
5. $\mathit{sqn}_s \leftarrow \mathit{sqn}_s + 1$ and $\nu_s \leftarrow C_n$; return $C = C_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, C_n$.

The chaining value is not transmitted [[RFC 2246, §6.2.3.2, Note]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#section-6.2.3.2), so $|C| = |R| + t + p + 1$, one block shorter than in TLS 1.1. This is the whole difference on the sending side: the first block's mask is not fresh, and it is the previous ciphertext block, which any eavesdropper has already seen.

##### 2.2.1.2 Decryption

![Receiver profiles](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition32-decryption-profiles.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.32 (decryption, two receiver profiles).</span> $\mathsf{Dec}^{X}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, C; \sigma)$ for a profile $X \in \{\mathrm{h}, \mathrm{r}\}$:

1. If $|C| \bmod b \ne 0$, or $n := |C|/b < 1$, or $nb < t + 1$, or $|C| > 2^{14} + 2048$, return $\bot$.
2. $C_0 \leftarrow \nu_r$; $P_i \leftarrow D_{K_e}(C_i) \oplus C_{i-1}$ for $i = 1, \dots, n$; set $P \leftarrow P_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, P_n$ and $\ell_P \leftarrow nb$.
3. $\mathit{ok} \leftarrow \Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}(P)$.
4. *Hardened profile* $\mathrm{h}$: $(R, T) \leftarrow \Phi_{1.1}(P)$; $v \leftarrow \big[\mathrm{MAC}_{K_a}\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}_r, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R\big) = T\big]$, computed on both branches; the result is $R$ if $\mathit{ok} \wedge v$ and $\bot$ otherwise. *RFC profile* $\mathrm{r}$: if $\neg \mathit{ok}$, the result is $\bot_{\mathrm{pad}}$ and no MAC is computed; otherwise $(R, T) \leftarrow \mathrm{strip}(P)$ and the result is $R$ if $v$, else $\bot_{\mathrm{mac}}$.
5. $\mathit{sqn}_r \leftarrow \mathit{sqn}_r + 1$ and $\nu_r \leftarrow C_n$; return the result.

Step 1 depends on $|C|$ alone, so both profiles write its rejection as $\bot$, although RFC 2246 sends $\mathtt{decryption\_failed}$ for a bad block multiple.

The RFC profile is [[RFC 2246]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246) as written: a padding failure gets its own fatal alert, $\mathtt{decryption\_failed}$, distinct from $\mathtt{bad\_record\_mac}$ [[RFC 2246, §7.2.2]](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2246#section-7.2.2), so $\bot_{\mathrm{pad}} \ne \bot_{\mathrm{mac}}$ is visible on the wire. The hardened profile is Definition 2.9 with the chaining value in place of the transmitted IV, which is what the Implementation Note of RFC 4346 retrofits onto TLS 1.0 receivers. Everything below is proved for the hardened profile; Lemma 2.48 carries it to the RFC profile.

<span style="color:#87A878">Proposition 2.33 (correctness).</span> For either profile, every key, header, record $R \in \mathcal{R}_{1.1}$ and state with $\mathit{sqn}_r = \mathit{sqn}_s$ and $\nu_r = \nu_s$, decryption of $\mathsf{Enc}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, R; \sigma)$ returns $R$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> As in Proposition 2.10, with $|C| = |R| + t + p + 1 \le 2^{14} + 276 < 2^{14} + 2048$ and $n \ge 1$, $nb \ge t + 1$. Step 2 reconstructs $P$ because $C_0 = \nu_r = \nu_s$ is the value the sender used, and the remaining blocks chain identically. The padding is well formed, so $\mathit{ok}$ holds, $r = |R|$, and the MAC inputs agree. The two profiles differ only on the paths not taken here. $\square$

##### 2.2.1.3 Leakage

![TLS 1.0 leakage](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition34-hmac-length.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.34 (HMAC input length).</span> For the hardened profile, $\lambda_{1.0}(P) = h + r$ with $r$ determined by $\Phi_{1.1}$, which is $\lambda_{1.1}$ of Definition 2.11 read on the TLS 1.0 plaintext; Models A and B apply with $\lambda$ replaced by $\lambda_{1.0}$ and $\mathsf{Dec}$ by Definition 2.32. For the RFC profile the MAC is skipped when $\mathit{ok}$ fails, so the count is $\varnothing$ on that branch, and the timing channel duplicates what the alert already reveals.

<span style="color:#87A878">Hypothesis 2.35 (on the receiver).</span> For the hardened profile, both clauses of Hypothesis 2.12 hold verbatim.


#### 2.2.2 IV-oblivious attacks against TLS 1.1 also break TLS 1.0

TLS 1.0 and TLS 1.1 are *not* equivalent, and no analogue of Theorem 2.24 holds. A TLS 1.1 decryption query carries its own first-block mask, the IV block; in TLS 1.0 that mask is the receiver's chaining value, which the adversary cannot set. The TLS 1.1 oracle is therefore the TLS 1.0 oracle plus control of one block, and the transfer runs one way only. The converse fails because TLS 1.0 has two weaknesses with no TLS 1.1 counterpart: the mask of the next record is public in advance, the predictable-IV setting that motivated the explicit IV, and the RFC profile's separate padding alert is a padding oracle in the sense of [[V02]](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/60268d2e-9c20-4d4f-9c85-6fff38c5454f/conferencedetails) and [[CHVV03]](https://www.scilit.com/publications/7a4b73573841857d9102de26df89dbd3) that needs no timing at all. This section proves the direction that holds: every TLS 1.1 attack of the shape built in §1 breaks TLS 1.0 with the same advantage and resources.

##### 2.2.2.1 The common parameter set

The parameter set is $\mathcal{S}$ of Definition 2.17, unchanged, with $h = 13$ by Definition 2.28; it contains the instantiation $b = 16$, $t = 20$ of §1. As in §2.1.2.1 the statements are made per algorithm pair.

##### 2.2.2.2 Scope: the record layer, with unseen chaining values

<span style="color:#87A878">Hypothesis 2.36 (unseen chaining value).</span> In each session, the chaining value $\nu_s$ that the sender uses for the challenge record is uniform on $\mathbb{B}^b$ and independent of the adversary's view. Nothing is assumed about $\nu_r$, the value the receiver holds when the adversary's query arrives: by clause (i) of Definition 2.46 the answer to that query does not depend on it.

The hypothesis is conservative. In the games, $\nu_s$ is $\nu_0$ or the last block of an earlier record that the adversary is never given; in deployment it is the last block of the preceding record, sent in clear, and granting it to the adversary could only improve the bounds below.

##### 2.2.2.3 Encoding

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.37 (encodings coincide).</span> For every $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$, every $R$ and every $T$, the TLS 1.0 encoding of Definition 2.30 is the TLS 1.1 encoding of Definition 2.4, hence by Lemma 2.19 the encoding $\mathsf{Encode}$ of Definition 1.5 with $j = (p - p_{\min}(R))/b$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Definition 2.30 is Definition 2.4 verbatim; the rest is Lemma 2.19. $\square$

##### 2.2.2.4 Encryption

![Chaining value as IV](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/lemma38-chaining-iv.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.38 (the chaining value is an IV).</span> Fix $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$ and a padding policy $\pi$. For every key, state, header $\mathrm{HDR} \in \mathcal{H}_{1.0}$ and record $R$, the ciphertext $\mathsf{Enc}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, R; \sigma)$ of Definition 2.31 is the non-IV part of the ciphertext that $\mathsf{Enc}^{1}_K\big(\psi_0(\mathrm{HDR}), R; (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r)\big)$ of Definition 2.6, instantiated with $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi_0$, produces when its IV draw returns $\nu_s$ and its policy returns the same $p$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> The tags agree by Lemma 2.40, the encodings by Lemma 2.37, step 3 of Definition 2.31 sets $C_0 = \nu_s$ where step 3 of Definition 2.6 draws it, and step 4 is the same chaining in both. Definition 2.31 then transmits $C_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, C_n$ and Definition 2.6 transmits $C_0 \,\|\, C_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, C_n$. $\square$

##### 2.2.2.5 Decryption

![Translations to TLS 1.1](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition39-translations.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.39 (the translations).</span> $\psi_0 : \mathcal{H}_{1.0} \to \mathcal{H}_{1.1}$ is the bijection $\mathit{type} \,\|\, \langle \texttt{0x0301} \rangle_2 \mapsto \mathit{type} \,\|\, \langle \texttt{0x0302} \rangle_2$, and $\phi_0 : \mathbb{B}^* \to \mathbb{B}^*$ is the map of Definition 2.14 built from the transposition of $\texttt{0x01}$ and $\texttt{0x02}$ instead of $\texttt{0x02}$ and $\texttt{0x03}$: it fixes every $x$ with $|x| \le 10$ and otherwise relabels the byte $x[10]$.

![TLS 1.0 and TLS 1.1 MAC inputs](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/lemma40-mac-inputs.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.40 (the MAC inputs correspond).</span> For every $\mathit{sqn}$, every $\mathrm{HDR} \in \mathcal{H}_{1.0}$ and every $R$ with $|R| < 2^{16}$, $\phi_0\big(\mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \psi_0(\mathrm{HDR}), R) \,\|\, R\big) = \mathrm{AD}(\mathit{sqn}, \mathrm{HDR}, R) \,\|\, R$, so $(\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi_0)_{K_a}$ on the first equals $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H_{K_a}$ on the second.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Identical to Lemma 2.15: byte $10$ is the version minor byte, $\texttt{0x01}$ for TLS 1.0 and $\texttt{0x02}$ for TLS 1.1, and the two strings agree elsewhere. $\square$

![Decryption with a supplied IV](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/lemma41-supplied-iv.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.41 (decryption is TLS 1.1 decryption with a supplied IV).</span> Fix $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$. For every key, state, header $\mathrm{HDR} \in \mathcal{H}_{1.0}$ and ciphertext $C \in \mathbb{B}^{nb}$ admitted by step 1 with $|C| \le 2^{14} + 2048 - b$,

$$\mathsf{Dec}^{\mathrm{h}}_K(\mathrm{HDR}, C; \sigma) \ = \ \mathsf{Dec}_K\big(\psi_0(\mathrm{HDR}), \nu_r \,\|\, C; (\mathit{sqn}_s, \mathit{sqn}_r)\big) ,$$

the right-hand side being Definition 2.9 instantiated with $\mathrm{HMAC}\text{-}H \circ \phi_0$, and the two computations produce the same $P$, the same $p$, the same $\mathit{ok}$, the same $r$ and the same $v$.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Step 1 of Definition 2.32 admits $C$ exactly when step 1 of Definition 2.9 admits $\nu_r \,\|\, C$: the block-multiple test is the same, the count of non-IV blocks is $n$ in both, the test $nb \ge t+1$ is the same, and the hypothesis $|C| \le 2^{14} + 2048 - b$ makes the two length caps agree, since $\nu_r \,\|\, C$ is one block longer than $C$. Step 2 of Definition 2.32 sets $C_0 = \nu_r$ and then computes $P_i = D_{K_e}(C_i) \oplus C_{i-1}$, which is step 2 of Definition 2.9 on $\nu_r \,\|\, C$. Steps 3 and 4 are $\Pi_{\mathrm{strict}}$, $\Phi_{1.1}$ and the MAC check of Definition 2.9, and the MAC values agree by Lemma 2.40. The returned value is $R$ iff $\mathit{ok} \wedge v$ in both. $\square$

The bound on $|C|$ excludes only the topmost block of lengths that Definition 2.32 admits, which no query below uses, every query being four blocks long. Definition 2.32 also advances $\nu_r$, but nothing reads it afterwards, since each session carries a single decryption query.

##### 2.2.2.6 Leakage

![IV-independent query](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition42-iv-independent.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.42 (IV-independent query).</span> A ciphertext $C = C_1 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, C_n$ with $n \ge 2$ is *IV-independent under $K$* if the pair $(\mathit{ok}, r)$ computed at steps 3 and 4 of Definition 2.32 is the same for every value of the chaining block $C_0 \in \mathbb{B}^b$.

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.43 (a sufficient condition).</span> The padding byte $p$ does not depend on $C_0$. If either the length test fails, $\ell_P < p + 1 + t$, or the candidate run fits in the last $n - 1$ blocks, $p + 1 \le (n-1)b$, then $C$ is IV-independent. In particular, at $b = 16$ and $t = 20$ every four-block ciphertext is IV-independent.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> $p$ is the last byte of $P_n = D_{K_e}(C_n) \oplus C_{n-1}$, which does not involve $C_0$ because $n \ge 2$. If the length test fails then $\mathit{ok}$ is false and $r = \ell_P - t$ whatever $C_0$ is. Otherwise $\mathit{ok}$ is decided by the last $p + 1$ bytes of $P$; if $p + 1 \le (n-1)b$ these lie in $P_2 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, P_n$, none of which involves $C_0$, and $r$ is then a function of $\ell_P$, $p$ and $\mathit{ok}$ alone. For the last claim, $\ell_P = 4b = 64$ and $t = 20$, so the length test passes only for $p \le 43$, and then $p + 1 \le 44 \le 48 = (n-1)b$. $\square$

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.44 (leakage coincides on IV-independent queries).</span> Let $C$ be IV-independent under $K$, admitted by step 1 and short enough for Lemma 2.41, $|C| \le 2^{14} + 2048 - b$. Then the value $\lambda_{1.0}(P)$ that Definition 2.34 attaches to the TLS 1.0 decryption of $C$ equals the value $\lambda_{1.1}(P')$ that Definition 2.11 attaches to the TLS 1.1 decryption of $C_0 \,\|\, C$, for every $C_0$; and $\mathsf{cost}_H$ of Proposition 1.13 maps them to the same count, since $\phi_0$ preserves length.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> By Lemma 2.41 the TLS 1.0 computation is the TLS 1.1 computation with $C_0 = \nu_r$, so $\lambda_{1.0}(P) = h + r$ for that value of $C_0$; by Definition 2.42 the same $r$ arises for every other value. $\square$

##### 2.2.2.7 Session model and games

![Games in TLS 1.0](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/lemma45-games.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.45 (the games instantiate).</span> Definitions 1.15, 1.16 and 1.17 are well defined for the TLS 1.0 scheme of §2.2.1 with the same parameters as for TLS 1.1, with one change of bookkeeping: a ciphertext has $n$ blocks rather than $n+1$, so the block that Definition 1.16 calls the $m$-th non-IV block of the ciphertext is here the $m$-th block, and $P^\ast$ is the same function of $R^\ast$. The predecessor block $C'$ of the target block is a transmitted ciphertext block exactly when $m \ge 2$; for $m = 1$ it is the chaining value, which Hypothesis 2.36 withholds.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Encryption, decryption and the leakage oracle are Definitions 2.31, 2.32 and 2.34; the sequence numbers start at $0$ and advance once per record, as in Definition 1.7; the encoding, and so the admissible target indices, coincide by Lemma 2.37. The statement about $C'$ is Definition 2.31: the block preceding the $m$-th is transmitted iff $m \ge 2$. $\square$

##### 2.2.2.8 The transfer theorem

![IV-oblivious adversary](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/definition46-iv-oblivious.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Definition 2.46 (IV-oblivious adversary).</span> An adversary $\mathcal{A}$ against $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$ in a game of $\mathcal{G}$ is *IV-oblivious* if: (i) every decryption query it makes has at least two non-IV blocks and is IV-independent under the session key; (ii) it never reads the IV block of a challenge ciphertext, using only the transmitted non-IV blocks; and (iii) its output is a function of the leakage components and of the ciphertexts it received, not of the verdicts.

![Transfer](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/theorem47-transfer.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Theorem 2.47 (transfer).</span> Let $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$, let $\pi$ be a deterministic policy, let $G \in \mathcal{G}$ and let $\mathcal{A}$ be IV-oblivious against $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$. Let $\mathcal{A}^{\downarrow}$ be the adversary against the TLS 1.0 scheme with the hardened profile that runs $\mathcal{A}$, relabels headers by $\psi_0$ and $\psi_0^{-1}$, deletes the IV block from each of $\mathcal{A}$'s queries before submitting it, and returns $\mathcal{A}$'s output. Then, under Hypotheses 2.29, 2.35 and 2.36,

$$\mathbf{Adv}^{G}_{\Pi_{1.0}^{\mathrm{h}}[E, H, \pi]}(\mathcal{A}^{\downarrow}, q) \ = \ \mathbf{Adv}^{G}_{\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]}(\mathcal{A}, q) ,$$

with the same number of sessions, one decryption query per session, each query one block shorter, and running time increased by one header relabelling per query.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Couple the two experiments on the session keys, the challenge records, the bit $d$ in the distinguishing games, the padding draws, the noise of each Model B query and the coins of $\mathcal{A}$, and couple the TLS 1.1 challenge IV of each session with the TLS 1.0 chaining value $\nu_s$ of that session, which Hypothesis 2.36 makes uniform and hidden on both sides. Under this coupling the challenge ciphertexts carry the same non-IV blocks, by Lemma 2.38 and Lemma 2.40, and clause (ii) says $\mathcal{A}$ reads nothing else. Its first query is therefore the same in both experiments, and $\mathcal{A}^{\downarrow}$ submits it without its IV block. By Lemma 2.41 the TLS 1.0 receiver computes what the TLS 1.1 receiver would compute on that query with the IV block replaced by $\nu_r$, and by clause (i) with Lemma 2.44 the pair $(\mathit{ok}, r)$, hence $\lambda$, hence the Model A count and the Model B measurement under the coupled noise, is the one $\mathcal{A}$ would have seen for its own choice of IV block. Clause (iii) makes the verdict components irrelevant to $\mathcal{A}$'s continuation, so its view is equal in the two experiments; the induction continues over the at most $q$ sessions, one query each. The restrictions of Definition 1.15 are met in the TLS 1.0 experiment exactly when they are met in the TLS 1.1 one, the ciphertexts differing only by the deleted block. Finally $\mathcal{A}$ outputs the same $\hat{P}$ or $\hat{d}$, and it is compared with the same $P^\ast$ by Lemma 2.45, or with the shared $d$. $\square$

<span style="color:#87A878">Lemma 2.48 (the RFC profile is at least as informative).</span> In Model A, every adversary against the hardened profile transfers to the RFC profile with the same advantage and the same resources.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> Given the RFC profile's answer to a query of ciphertext length $nb$, the hardened profile's answer is computed without further queries. The verdict maps $\bot_{\mathrm{pad}} \mapsto \bot$, $\bot_{\mathrm{mac}} \mapsto \bot$ and $R \mapsto R$. For the count: the answer $\bot_{\mathrm{pad}}$ says exactly that $\mathit{ok}$ is false, in which case the hardened profile computes the MAC on $\mathrm{strip}_0$, whose count is $\mathsf{cost}_H(h + nb - t)$, a function of the public length alone; and on any other answer $\mathit{ok}$ holds, both profiles take the same branch, and the RFC profile's own count is the hardened one. $\square$

The RFC profile is in fact strictly more informative: $\bot_{\mathrm{pad}}$ reveals $\mathit{ok}$ with no timing measurement at all. This is the verdict-level padding oracle of [[V02]](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/60268d2e-9c20-4d4f-9c85-6fff38c5454f/conferencedetails), and plaintext recovery against a receiver following RFC 2246 as written follows from it with no side channel. It is not literally the primitive of Corollary 2.49: $\mathsf{Probe}$ tests Case 2 of §1.2.2, well-formed padding of length at least two, whereas the alert separates Cases 1 and 2 together from Case 3.


#### 2.2.3 Attacks against TLS 1.1 also break TLS 1.0

![Attack transfer to TLS 1.0](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/articles/post-quantum-algorithms/TLS-10-11/illustrations/corollary49-attack-transfer.svg)

<span style="color:#87A878">Corollary 2.49 (TLS 1.0 offers no confidentiality either).</span> Let $(E, H) \in \mathcal{S}$ with $b = 16$ and $t = 20$, let $\pi$ be deterministic, let the receiver satisfy Hypothesis 2.35, and let the target block not be the first block of its record. Then under Hypotheses 2.29 and 2.36, in Model A, the adversary $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{\downarrow}$ recovers the target block of the TLS 1.0 scheme with advantage exactly $1$, under no cryptographic assumption, from at most $M = 2^{16} + 43 + 14 \cdot 2^{8}$ sessions of one decryption query each; in Model B it succeeds with probability at least $1 - \delta$ at $L \ge (8 \sigma_{\!\eta}^2/c_0^2) \ln (2M/\delta)$ repetitions per mask. In Model A the same holds against the RFC profile, by Lemma 2.48, although there the alert alone already suffices, as remarked after Lemma 2.48.

<span style="color:#808080">*Proof.*</span> By Corollary 2.26, $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}$ attains these advantages against $\Pi_{1.1}[E, H, \pi]$. It is IV-oblivious. For (i), its queries are the four-block forgeries $C^{\mathrm{att}}(\Delta)$ of §1.2.1, which are IV-independent by Lemma 2.43. For (ii), it reads from each session only the target block $C^\ast_s$ and its predecessor $C'_s$, both transmitted blocks by Lemma 2.45 since the target is not the first block, and the IV block $C_0$ of its own forgery is arbitrary, being fixed in advance in Algorithm 1.18. For (iii), $\mathsf{Probe}$ returns $[c = 4]$, a function of the count alone. Theorem 2.47 therefore gives $\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{PR}}^{\downarrow}$ against the hardened profile with the same advantage and the same session count, and Lemma 2.48 carries it to the RFC profile. $\square$

TLS 1.1 and TLS 1.2 CBC mode are the same scheme up to a version byte (Theorem 2.24). TLS 1.0 is not, but every IV-oblivious attack on TLS 1.1, Lucky Thirteen included, breaks it too (Theorem 2.47), and a receiver following RFC 2246 as written falls to the padding alert alone. Moving from TLS 1.0 to TLS 1.2 CBC mode closes the predictable-IV attacks and the alert-based padding oracle, but not the timing channel that all three versions share.
