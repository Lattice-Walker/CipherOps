#  Quality of Service and Congestion Management

## 1 Network Congestion

Congestion occurs when the aggregate demand for a network resource exceeds its available capacity. This resource may be link bandwidth, a router's processing capacity, or the buffer space allocated to a queue.

Congestion arises from several distinct causes. Bandwidth bottlenecks occur when a link along a path has less capacity than the links feeding into it, forcing traffic to queue or be discarded. Traffic bursts can temporarily exceed a link's capacity even if the long-term average utilisation of that link remains moderate. Asymmetric link speeds between network segments, such as a high-speed core connecting to a lower-speed access link, produce a similar effect at the point where the mismatch occurs.

A specific case of bursty traffic is the microburst, a short-duration spike that exceeds available buffer capacity even when utilisation measured over longer intervals, such as one second or more, appears well within acceptable limits. Because standard monitoring tools often sample at intervals too coarse to detect microbursts, they can cause packet loss that is not reflected in aggregate utilisation statistics.

The effects of congestion on traffic include increased queuing delay as packets wait for available capacity, packet loss once buffers overflow, and additional load generated by TCP retransmissions of the lost packets. In severe cases, this can lead to a condition described by Van Jacobson in 1988 as TCP congestion collapse, in which retransmissions from multiple flows consume an increasing share of a link's capacity, further reducing the capacity available for useful throughput.

UDP does not include a built-in congestion backoff mechanism equivalent to TCP's congestion control algorithms. As a result, UDP flows sharing a congested link with TCP flows can continue transmitting at their original rate while TCP flows reduce theirs, allowing UDP traffic to consume a disproportionate share of available capacity.

Head-of-line blocking is a related effect in which a single large or delayed packet at the front of a queue prevents subsequent packets from being processed, even if those later packets could otherwise be forwarded without delay.

Congestion can occur at multiple layers of the network stack: in switch buffers at Layer 2, in router queues at Layer 3, and in application-layer buffers such as those used for streaming media. Common indicators used to measure or detect congestion include interface utilisation, queue depth, packet drop counters, and increases in round-trip time.

### 1.1 Buffer Management

Buffers absorb short-term traffic bursts, allowing packets to be queued temporarily rather than discarded immediately when a link is momentarily oversubscribed.

The default queue behaviour on most network devices is tail drop, in which packets arriving after a queue has reached its maximum size are discarded, without regard to which flow they belong to or their relative priority. Tail drop can produce a phenomenon known as TCP global synchronisation: when a shared queue fills, packets from multiple TCP flows are dropped at approximately the same time, causing all affected flows to reduce their congestion window simultaneously. This is followed by a period of underutilisation as the flows ramp back up together, then another synchronised burst, producing oscillating link utilisation.

Random Early Detection addresses this problem by dropping packets probabilistically before the queue becomes full, based on the average queue length. This signals congestion to TCP senders earlier and in a distributed manner, reducing the likelihood that many flows reduce their window at the same time.

Weighted Random Early Detection extends this mechanism by applying different drop thresholds to different traffic classes, so that higher-priority traffic experiences a lower probability of early drop than lower-priority traffic under the same congestion conditions.

Explicit Congestion Notification, defined in RFC 3168, provides an alternative to packet dropping as a congestion signal. Routers experiencing congestion set specific bits in the IP header, which are then reflected by the receiving TCP endpoint back to the sender, indicating that the sender should reduce its transmission rate without the network having to discard a packet to convey this information.

A separate issue, bufferbloat, was identified and named by Jim Gettys around 2010. Bufferbloat refers to the effect of excessively large buffers, which absorb bursts without dropping packets but in doing so allow queuing delay to grow substantially, introducing high and variable latency without any corresponding improvement in throughput. Active Queue Management algorithms developed to address bufferbloat, including CoDel and FQ-CoDel, use queuing delay itself, rather than queue length, as the variable controlling when to drop packets.

Buffer sizing therefore involves a trade-off: smaller buffers keep queuing delay low but increase the drop rate under bursty traffic, while larger buffers absorb bursts more effectively but at the cost of higher queuing delay when the buffer is occupied.

## 2 Quality of Service

### 2.1 Traffic Prioritisation

Quality of Service refers to the practice of treating different traffic flows differently based on their characteristics or an assigned traffic class, rather than forwarding all traffic identically. The rationale is that different types of traffic have different tolerances for latency, jitter, and loss: voice and video traffic generally require low latency and low jitter, while bulk file transfers can tolerate greater delay without a noticeable effect on the outcome.

Applying differentiated treatment requires classification, the process of identifying traffic based on attributes such as source or destination IP address, port number, protocol, Differentiated Services Code Point marking, or, in more granular implementations, deep packet inspection of payload content.

Once classified, traffic is typically marked with a class identifier at the point it enters the network, so that downstream devices can apply consistent treatment based on the marking alone, without needing to re-classify the traffic at every hop. The point at which markings originating from end devices are either accepted or overridden is referred to as a trust boundary, and is typically located at the access layer, close to where traffic enters the network from end-user equipment.

Once classified, packets are handled according to a queuing discipline, which determines how they are scheduled for transmission. First In First Out is the default behaviour, providing no differentiation between traffic types. Priority Queuing applies strict priority between classes, transmitting all traffic in a higher-priority queue before any traffic in a lower-priority queue is sent, which creates a risk of starvation for lower-priority traffic if higher-priority queues are continuously occupied. Weighted Fair Queuing allocates bandwidth proportionally across flows according to assigned weights. Class-Based Weighted Fair Queuing applies this same proportional allocation to defined traffic classes rather than to individual flows. Low Latency Queuing combines a strict-priority queue, generally reserved for latency-sensitive traffic such as voice, with weighted fair queuing applied to the remaining traffic classes.

Two further mechanisms operate on traffic that exceeds a defined rate: shaping delays excess traffic, holding it in a buffer until it can be transmitted within the conforming rate, while policing drops or re-marks excess traffic immediately upon detection, without delaying it.

A related distinction is drawn between congestion avoidance, which acts before queues become full, such as Random Early Detection, and congestion management, which acts on traffic that has already accumulated in a queue, such as the queuing disciplines described above.

### 2.2 QoS Models

The default model on the internet is Best Effort, in which all packets are treated equally and no guarantees are made regarding delay, loss, or available bandwidth.

Integrated Services, defined in RFC 1633, was developed to provide guarantees for individual traffic flows. It uses the Resource Reservation Protocol to request and maintain bandwidth and delay guarantees for a specific flow along its path. Because Integrated Services requires every router along the path to maintain state for each individual flow, it does not scale to networks carrying large numbers of concurrent flows, which has limited its deployment.

Differentiated Services, defined in RFC 2474 and RFC 2475, was developed as a more scalable alternative. Rather than maintaining per-flow state, routers apply a Per-Hop Behaviour to each packet based on the value of the Differentiated Services Code Point field carried in the IP header, allowing traffic to be handled according to its class rather than its individual flow.

Several standard Per-Hop Behaviours are defined for use with Differentiated Services Code Point marking. Default Forwarding corresponds to best-effort treatment. Expedited Forwarding is intended for traffic requiring low latency, low loss, and low jitter, such as voice. Assured Forwarding defines four traffic classes, each with three drop precedence levels, providing relative differentiation in the likelihood of drop within a class during congestion. Class Selector marking preserves backward compatibility with the older IP Precedence field used before the definition of Differentiated Services.

Integrated Services and Differentiated Services represent different trade-offs: Integrated Services provides hard, per-flow guarantees but does not scale to large networks, while Differentiated Services scales to large networks but provides only relative differentiation between classes rather than guarantees for individual flows.

Achieving consistent Quality of Service from end to end requires that a uniform policy be applied across every network domain a packet traverses. This is difficult to enforce when a path crosses multiple administrative boundaries, such as when traffic passes from an enterprise network through one or more carrier networks before reaching its destination, since each domain may apply its own classification and marking policies.

### 2.3 MPLS

Multiprotocol Label Switching is a forwarding mechanism in which packets are assigned a short, fixed-length label at the point they enter a network domain and are then forwarded at each subsequent hop based on that label, rather than through a routing lookup based on the destination IP address. It was standardised by the Internet Engineering Task Force, with the core architecture defined in RFC 3031 and the label stack encoding defined in RFC 3032.

An MPLS label consists of a 20-bit label value, a 3-bit Traffic Class field, a 1-bit bottom-of-stack indicator used when multiple labels are stacked, and an 8-bit time-to-live field.

Within an MPLS domain, a Label Edge Router is positioned at the boundary of the domain and is responsible for imposing a label on packets entering the domain or removing it from packets leaving the domain. A Label Switch Router operates within the core of the domain and forwards packets based solely on the label, without examining the IP header.

Label bindings, the mapping between labels and the paths they represent, are distributed between routers using either the Label Distribution Protocol or RSVP-TE, an extension of the Resource Reservation Protocol. RSVP-TE additionally supports traffic engineering through explicit control over the path a label-switched path follows, rather than relying solely on the path selected by the interior gateway protocol.

This traffic engineering capability allows network operators to route specific flows along paths that differ from the shortest path computed by the interior gateway protocol, which can be used to balance load across multiple links or to reserve bandwidth along a specific path for particular traffic.

MPLS is also used to construct MPLS-based Layer 3 VPNs, defined in RFC 4364, in which label stacks are used to isolate the traffic of different customers sharing a common provider backbone.

The Traffic Class field within the MPLS label can carry an equivalent of a Differentiated Services Per-Hop Behaviour, allowing Quality of Service policies to be applied consistently to labelled traffic as it passes through an MPLS domain.

MPLS is generally described as a carrier-grade technology, historically used by enterprises to obtain predictable performance for wide area network connectivity. Growth in broadband availability and the adoption of SD-WAN, which uses encrypted overlays across internet-based transport to achieve some of the same connectivity goals, has reduced demand among enterprises for dedicated MPLS circuits. In adopting internet-based alternatives, enterprises generally accept different reliability and Quality of Service characteristics than those provided by a dedicated MPLS circuit. MPLS itself remains in widespread use within carrier backbones, independently of its declining use as an enterprise access technology.
