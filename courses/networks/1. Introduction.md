# 1. Introduction to Networks and Cybersecurity

## 1.1 What Is a Computer Network?

[NIST SP 800-171R3](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/NIST.SP.800-171r3.pdf) defines a network as 
> A system implemented with a collection of interconnected components. Such components may include routers, hubs, cabling, telecommunications controllers, key distribution centers, and technical control devices.
Its primary purposes are to allow communication between users and systems, to allow multiple devices to share hardware and software ressources, and to enable access to centralised or distributed data storage.


The history of modern networking began in the 1960s with ARPANET, a packet-switched network developed by the US Department of Defense. In the 1970s, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn developed the TCP/IP protocols, which became the foundation for internet communication. The 1980s saw local area networks expand across enterprises, while the 1990s brought the World Wide Web, introduced by Tim Berners-Lee, and the opening of the internet to the general public. Broadband, Wi-Fi, and mobile connectivity spread widely during the 2000s. From the 2010s onward, cloud computing, the Internet of Things, and 5G networks have continued to reshape how devices and systems communicate.

Nowadays, two fundamental architectural models define how devices interact within a network: the Client-Server Model, and the Peer-to-Peer Model.
| Feature | Client-Server | Peer-to-Peer |
|---|---|---|
| **Structure** | Central server provides services; clients send requests and receive responses | All devices have equal status and act as both client and server |
| **Control** | Centralised | Distributed, no central authority |
| **Management** | Easier to manage at scale | Harder to manage and secure at scale |
| **Security enforcement** | Easier to enforce | More difficult to enforce |
| **Single point of failure** | Yes | No |
| **Setup cost** | Higher | Lower |
| **Typical examples** | Web browsing, corporate email systems | BitTorrent, early file-sharing networks, some blockchain architectures |

Beyond the choice of architectural model, the physical or logical arrangement of devices within a network, referred to as its topology, also shapes how data flows and how the network responds to failures. The main topology types are bus, star, ring, mesh, and hybrid.

![Network Topology](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1Topology.svg)

Networks vary in scale and purpose depending on the environment in which they operate. In a home setting, a Local Area Network connects devices such as smartphones, laptops, and smart televisions through a router. Enterprises rely on Local and Wide Area Networks to link offices, servers, and employees across internal infrastructure. Universities typically use Metropolitan or Wireless Local Area Networks to connect buildings, laboratories, and student devices across a campus. At the largest scale, the internet is a global Wide Area Network interconnecting billions of devices worldwide. The banking sector uses private Wide Area Networks to maintain secure, dedicated connections between branches and data centers. In healthcare, intranets and Virtual Private Networks allow hospitals to share patient records across isolated, secured environments.

![Geographic Scope](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1GeographicScope.svg)


Computer networks connect people, devices, and systems regardless of physical distance, allowing organisations to operate across multiple locations without relying on direct physical contact between users or machines. By sharing hardware such as printers and servers, as well as software licences, networks reduce the need for duplicate equipment and lower overall infrastructure costs.

Security management becomes more straightforward on a networked environment, as administrators can enforce access policies, apply updates, and monitor for threats from a central point rather than managing each device individually. Redundancy mechanisms, such as backup links or failover systems, similarly reduce the likelihood that a single point of failure will bring down an entire system. Networks can also be expanded by adding new devices, users, or nodes as an organisation grows, without requiring a complete redesign of the existing infrastructure.

### 1.1.1 Transmission Media, Bandwidth, and Latency
Network communication depends on three interrelated concepts: the medium carrying the data, the capacity of that medium, and the delays introduced during transmission.

Transmission media are either wired or wireless. Wired options include Ethernet, fiber optic cable, and coaxial cable; wireless options include Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and cellular networks. Wired connections generally offer more stable throughput and lower latency, while wireless connections allow greater mobility and easier deployment in some environments.

The capacity of a medium is described by its bandwidth, which is the maximum data rate a channel can carry, measured in bits per second and its multiples: kilobits, megabits, and gigabits per second. In practice, the actual rate achieved, called throughput, is lower than the theoretical bandwidth due to congestion, protocol overhead, and hardware limitations. A connection advertised at 100 Mbps, for instance, may sustain a real throughput of 70–80 Mbps under normal load.

Even when throughput is adequate, delays can affect performance. Latency is the time between a sender transmitting a signal and the receiver receiving it, measured in milliseconds, and it is shaped by propagation delay, transmission delay, and queuing delay in routers. Jitter is the variation in latency between successive packets. A consistently high latency can sometimes be tolerated by an application, but irregular jitter causes noticeable disruption in real-time audio and video, since packets no longer arrive at their expected intervals.

### 1.1.2 Network Protocols
A network protocol is a set of rules that governs how data is formatted, transmitted, and received between devices. Several protocols operate together in a typical network, each handling a distinct function.

The most fundamental pairing is TCP/IP. The Internet Protocol handles addressing and routing, breaking data into packets and directing each one toward its destination. The Transmission Control Protocol sits above it and ensures reliable delivery: it establishes a connection before data is sent, numbers each packet, and requests retransmission of any that are lost or corrupted. Where reliability matters less than speed, the User Datagram Protocol offers an alternative: it sends packets without establishing a connection or confirming receipt, which reduces overhead and suits applications such as video streaming, voice calls, and online gaming.

Above the transport layer, the Hypertext Transfer Protocol governs how web browsers and servers exchange data. A browser requests a resource, and the server responds with the content or an error code. Most modern web traffic uses the secured version of this protocol, which encrypts the exchange using TLS.

Two further protocols manage background functions that most users never interact with directly. The Domain Name System translates human-readable domain names into the numerical IP addresses that routers use to forward traffic. The Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol automatically assigns IP addresses to devices when they join a network, removing the need for manual configuration.

### 1.1.3 The OCI and TCP/IP Models
This section introduces the two principal models used to describe network communication. Both are covered in greater depth in [Section 2, Network Models, Layers and Protocols](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/display_courses/courses.html#article=courses%2Fnetworks%2F2.%20Network%20Models.md).

![OSI vs TCP IP](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1OSITCPIP.svg)

#### The OSI Model
The OSI model was developed to provide a universal framework for network communication, independent of any particular technology or vendor. Its seven layers are numbered from the bottom up, where the lower layers deal with physical signal transmission and the upper layers deal with application-level data exchange.

- **Layer 1**, the physical layer, is concerned with the raw transmission of bits over a medium.
    - Defines the electrical, optical, or radio signals used, as well as the physical characteristics of cables, connectors, and network interfaces.
    - Has no awareness of what the bits represent.
- **Layer 2**, the data link layer, takes the raw bit stream from the physical layer and organises it into frames.
    - Handles addressing at the local network level using MAC addresses, and it manages access to the shared medium so that devices do not transmit simultaneously and corrupt each other's signals.
    - Ethernet and Wi-Fi both operate at this layer.
- **Layer 3**, the network layer, is responsible for logical addressing and routing.
    - Assigns IP addresses to devices and determines the path that packets take across multiple networks to reach their destination.
    - Routers operate at this layer.
- **Layer 4**, the transport layer, manages end-to-end communication between two devices.
    - Segments data coming from the application layer into smaller units, numbers them so they can be reassembled in order, and handles error checking and flow control.
    - TCP and UDP both operate at this layer: TCP provides reliable, ordered delivery, while UDP prioritises speed over reliability.
- **Layer 5**, the session layer, establishes, maintains, and terminates communication sessions between applications.
    - Coordinates which side transmits at a given moment and can resume a session if the connection is interrupted.
- **Layer 6**, the presentation layer, is responsible for translating data between the format used by the application and the format used by the network.
    - This includes character encoding, data compression, and encryption.
    - TLS encryption, for example, is often associated with this layer.
- **Layer 7**, the application layer, is the one closest to the user.
    - Provides the interface through which software accesses network services.
    - Protocols such as HTTP, DNS, and DHCP operate here.

#### The TCP/IP Model
The TCP/IP model was developed alongside the protocols that power the internet and reflects how those protocols are actually implemented. It has four layers, each of which corresponds to one or more layers of the OSI model.

- The network access layer combines the functions of OSI layers 1 and 2. It handles the physical transmission of data and local addressing within a single network segment, covering everything from the electrical signal on a cable to the framing of data for local delivery.
- The network layer corresponds directly to OSI layer 3. It handles IP addressing and the routing of packets between networks.
- The transport layer corresponds to OSI layer 4 and serves the same function: managing end-to-end delivery using either TCP or UDP depending on the requirements of the application.
- The application layer consolidates OSI layers 5, 6, and 7. In practice, modern application protocols handle session management, data formatting, and user-facing functionality without treating these as separate concerns, which is why the TCP/IP model merges them into a single layer.

#### Encapsulation and Decapsulation
When data is sent across a network, it passes downward through the layers on the sending device. At each layer, a header (and in some cases a trailer) is added to the data, wrapping it with the information that layer needs to do its job. This process is called encapsulation. The unit of data at each layer has a specific name: at the transport layer it is called a segment (TCP) or datagram (UDP), at the network layer it becomes a packet, and at the data link layer it becomes a frame.

On the receiving device, the process runs in reverse. Each layer reads and removes the header added by its counterpart on the sending side, passing the remaining data upward until the original payload reaches the application. This is called decapsulation.

This layered approach means that each layer only needs to understand its own header and can treat everything above it as opaque data. A router, for instance, reads the network layer header to determine where to forward a packet, but does not inspect the transport or application data inside it.
    
### 1.1.4 Network Devices

A network is built from a combination of devices, each placed at a specific point in the architecture to perform a distinct function. Understanding what each device does and where it sits helps explain how traffic flows from a user's machine to a remote server and back.

#### Hub

A hub is the simplest device for connecting multiple machines within a local network. When it receives a signal on one port, it repeats that signal out of every other port, regardless of which device the data is intended for. Every device on the hub therefore receives every transmission, which wastes bandwidth and creates collisions when two devices transmit at the same time. Hubs operate at the physical layer and have largely been replaced by switches in modern networks.

![HUB](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1HUB.svg)
#### Switch

A switch connects devices within a local network in the same physical arrangement as a hub, but operates more intelligently. It maintains a table mapping each port to the MAC address of the device connected to it, and uses this table to forward a frame only to the port where the destination device is located. This reduces unnecessary traffic and eliminates most collisions. Switches operate at the data link layer.

![SWITCH](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1SWITCH.svg)
#### Router

A router connects separate networks, most commonly a local network to the internet. Where a switch uses MAC addresses to forward frames within a single network, a router uses IP addresses to forward packets between networks. It examines the destination IP address of each incoming packet, consults its routing table, and sends the packet toward its destination via the most appropriate path. Routers operate at the network layer.

![ROUTER](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1ROUTER.svg)
#### Firewall

A firewall monitors and filters network traffic according to a set of rules, permitting or blocking packets based on criteria such as source address, destination address, port number, and protocol. It is typically placed at the boundary between a private network and the internet, though firewalls can also be deployed internally to separate segments of a larger network. Some firewalls operate at the network layer, inspecting packet headers; more advanced ones operate at the application layer and can inspect the content of traffic.

![FIREWALL](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1FIREWALL.svg)
#### Access Point

A wireless access point connects wireless devices to a wired network. It broadcasts a Wi-Fi signal that nearby devices can associate with, and bridges the wireless traffic onto the wired local network. In a home setting, the access point is usually integrated into the same device as the router and modem. In larger deployments, dedicated access points are distributed across a building and connected back to a central switch.

![ACCESS POINT](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1AP.svg)
#### Modem

A modem converts between the digital signals used by a local network and the signal format used by the provider's infrastructure, whether that is a telephone line, a cable television line, or a fibre connection. The word is a contraction of modulator-demodulator, reflecting this conversion in both directions. In home networks, the modem is the point where the private network meets the provider's network, and it is usually connected directly to the router.

![MODEM](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1MODEM.svg)
#### Position in a Typical Network Architecture

These devices do not operate in isolation; they occupy specific positions in a layered architecture. A typical small network places the modem at the outermost edge, connected to the internet via the provider's infrastructure. The router sits immediately behind it, separating the public and private address spaces and directing traffic between them. A firewall may be integrated into the router or deployed as a separate device at this boundary. Inside the private network, one or more switches connect wired devices, and one or more access points extend connectivity to wireless devices.

![Full Architecture](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1FULLARCHITECTURE.svg)


### 1.1.5 IP Addressing

Every device on a network requires a unique identifier so that traffic can be directed to the correct destination. This identifier is the IP address, and it functions in much the same way as a postal address: it tells the network where a packet originates and where it should be delivered.

#### IPv4 and IPv6

The original addressing scheme, IPv4, represents addresses as four groups of numbers separated by dots, each ranging from 0 to 255, for example 192.168.1.1. This format provides approximately 4.3 billion unique addresses, a figure that seemed sufficient in the early days of networking but proved inadequate as the number of connected devices grew. IPv6 was developed in response. It represents addresses as eight groups of four hexadecimal digits separated by colons, for example `2001:0db8:85a3:0000:0000:8a2e:0370:7334`, and provides a vastly larger address space: approximately 340 undecillion addresses. IPv6 also simplifies certain aspects of address configuration and removes the need for some workarounds that IPv4 required. Both versions coexist on most modern networks, with many systems supporting both simultaneously.

![IPV4 and IPv6](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1ipv4ipv6.svg)


#### Public and Private Addresses

Not every device on a network needs an address that is reachable from the internet. IPv4 reserves certain address ranges for use within private networks, including `10.0.0.0` to `10.255.255.255`, `172.16.0.0` to `172.31.255.255`, and `192.168.0.0` to `192.168.255.255`. These addresses can be reused freely across different private networks because they are never routed on the public internet. A device using a private address communicates with the outside world through a router that holds a public address, which is one assigned by an internet service provider and reachable from anywhere on the internet.

![Public and Private addresses](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1publicprivate.svg)

#### Subnetting and CIDR Notation

A subnet is a subdivision of a network, created by partitioning a block of IP addresses into smaller groups. This allows an organisation to separate traffic between departments, limit broadcast domains, and apply different security policies to different parts of the network. Subnets are defined using a subnet mask, which specifies how many bits of an IP address identify the network and how many identify individual hosts within it. CIDR notation expresses this concisely by appending a slash and a number to the address: in 192.168.1.0/24, the /24 indicates that the first 24 bits identify the network, leaving 8 bits for host addresses and allowing up to 254 usable addresses in that subnet. Shortening the prefix, for example to /25 or /26, splits the address space into a greater number of smaller subnets.

![Network and Host](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1networkportionhostportion.svg)

In practice, subnetting means dividing a single address block into separate segments, each served by its own switch and covering a distinct range of host addresses. The diagram below shows a /24 block divided into four /26 subnets, each with its own usable host range and broadcast address.

![Subnetting](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1subnetting.svg)


#### Network Address Translation

Network Address Translation is a mechanism that allows multiple devices sharing a single public IP address to communicate with the internet. When a device on a private network sends a packet outward, the router replaces the private source address with its own public address and records the mapping in a translation table. When a response arrives, the router consults that table and forwards the packet to the correct internal device. This approach extended the usable life of IPv4 by allowing thousands of devices to share a small number of public addresses, and it remains common in home and enterprise networks even as IPv6 adoption continues.

![Translation](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1translation.svg)




## 1.2 Cybersecurity

The National Institute of Standards and Technology defines cybersecurity in its [Glossary of Key Information Security Terms](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.7298r3.pdf) as the prevention of damage to, protection of, and restoration of computers, electronic communications systems, and electronic communications services, to ensure their availability, integrity, authentication, confidentiality, and nonrepudiation. The [NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.29.pdf) organises the discipline around six core functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover.

[ISO/IEC 27000:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/73906.html) defines information security as the preservation of confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information, and notes that additional properties such as authenticity, accountability, and non-repudiation may also be relevant. And [ISO/IEC 27032](https://www.iso.org/standard/82875.html) extends this to cyberspace specifically, covering threats that originate in or propagate through internet-connected environments.

The terms cybersecurity, information security, and IT security are often used interchangeably in practice, but they carry distinct meanings in standardised terminology. Information security, as defined in ISO/IEC 27000:2018, is the broader category: it covers the protection of information in any form, including physical documents, verbal communication, and digital data, against unauthorised access, disclosure, alteration, or destruction. IT security refers specifically to the protection of information technology systems, that is, the hardware, software, and networks that store and process information. Cybersecurity, as positioned in ISO/IEC 27032, focuses on threats and risks that originate in or propagate through cyberspace, including attacks on internet-connected systems, and extends to concerns such as online safety and the security of interactions between individuals and digital services.

In practical terms, information security is the widest of the three concepts, IT security is a subset focused on technical systems, and cybersecurity addresses the specific risk environment created by interconnected digital networks. These boundaries are not always observed consistently across legislation, industry standards, or organisational policy, which is a recognised source of terminological ambiguity in the field.

#### Key objectives

Cybersecurity practice is generally organised around four operational objectives. Prevention refers to measures taken to reduce the likelihood of a security incident, including access controls, patch management, network segmentation, and security awareness training. Detection covers the processes and tools used to identify security events as they occur or shortly after, such as intrusion detection systems, log monitoring, and anomaly analysis. Response encompasses the actions taken once an incident is confirmed, including containment, eradication of the threat, and communication to relevant stakeholders. Recovery refers to the restoration of affected systems and services to normal operation, along with the documentation and analysis needed to reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

![Key objectives](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1keyobjectives.svg)

These four objectives correspond directly to functions defined in the [NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.29.pdf), which structures organisational cybersecurity activity around Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover, with Govern added in the 2024 revision to reflect the role of organisational policy and risk management in supporting all other functions.

#### Cybersecurity domains
Cybersecurity is applied across a range of distinct technical and organisational domains, each addressing a specific category of assets or infrastructure.

- **Network security** covers the protection of communication infrastructure, including routers, switches, firewalls, and the protocols that govern data transmission, against unauthorised access, interception, and disruption.
- **Application security** addresses vulnerabilities introduced during the design, development, and deployment of software. It includes practices such as secure coding, code review, and penetration testing, and applies to both web and mobile applications.
- **Endpoint security focuses on individual devices that connect to a network, including workstations, laptops, and mobile devices. It encompasses antivirus software, device encryption, and policies governing device access and configuration.
- **Cloud security** concerns the protection of data, applications, and infrastructure hosted in cloud environments. It addresses access management, data residency, shared responsibility models between cloud providers and their customers, and compliance with applicable regulations.
- **Operational security**, commonly referred to as OpSec, is a process-oriented domain concerned with identifying and protecting sensitive information that could be exploited by an adversary. It originated in military practice and has been adopted in civilian cybersecurity to govern how information is handled, shared, and stored within an organisation.
- **Disaster recovery and business continuity** cover the planning and procedures that allow an organisation to maintain or rapidly resume operations following a disruptive event, whether caused by a cyberattack, hardware failure, or other incident. Disaster recovery focuses on the restoration of IT systems and data, while business continuity addresses the broader operational continuity of the organisation during and after a disruption.

![Cyber domains](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1cyberdomains.svg)

#### Actors in cybersecurity

Cybersecurity involves two broad categories of actors: those who attempt to compromise systems and those who defend them.

Threat actors are individuals or groups that carry out or attempt to carry out attacks against information systems. They vary considerably in motivation, capability, and resources. Criminal organisations typically seek financial gain through methods such as ransomware, fraud, or data theft. Nation-state actors operate on behalf of governments and tend to pursue strategic objectives such as espionage, sabotage, or the disruption of critical infrastructure. Insider threats originate from within an organisation, whether from employees acting maliciously or from individuals who inadvertently expose systems through negligence or error. Hacktivists conduct attacks to advance political or ideological goals, while opportunistic attackers exploit known vulnerabilities without specific targets in mind.

Defenders are the individuals and teams responsible for protecting systems, detecting incidents, and coordinating responses. Security Operations Center teams monitor networks and systems continuously, analyse alerts, and respond to confirmed incidents. The Chief Information Security Officer is the senior organisational role responsible for defining and overseeing the security strategy, managing risk, and ensuring compliance with relevant regulations and standards. Security analysts investigate alerts and incidents, conduct threat hunting, and produce assessments that inform defensive decisions. In larger organisations, these roles are supported by incident response teams, threat intelligence analysts, and security engineers responsible for designing and maintaining defensive infrastructure.

#### Economic and social stakes
Cybersecurity incidents carry measurable costs across financial, reputational, and national security dimensions.

Financially, the direct costs of an incident include incident response, system restoration, legal fees, regulatory fines, and compensation to affected parties. Indirect costs include lost revenue during downtime and the longer-term effects of customer attrition. IBM's [Cost of a Data Breach Report 2024](https://www.ibm.com/reports/data-breach) estimated the global average cost of a data breach at 4.88 million USD, the highest figure recorded since the report's inception.

Reputational damage is harder to quantify but can affect an organisation for years after an incident. Public disclosure of a breach, particularly one involving personal or financial data, tends to reduce customer trust and can influence stock valuations in the case of publicly listed companies.

At the national level, attacks on critical infrastructure, including energy grids, water systems, financial networks, and healthcare facilities, carry implications that extend beyond individual organisations. Several governments now classify certain categories of cyberattack as matters of national security, and incidents attributed to nation-state actors have prompted diplomatic and legislative responses in multiple jurisdictions.

### 1.2.1 The CIA Triad

The CIA Triad is the foundational model of information security, grouping its core objectives into three properties: confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The model is referenced across major security frameworks, including [ISO/IEC 27000:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/73906.html) and the [NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/CSWP/NIST.CSWP.29.pdf), which build their definitions of information security around these three properties.

![CIA triad](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1CIA.svg)

- **Confidentiality** is the property that information is not made available or disclosed to unauthorised individuals, entities, or processes, as defined in [ISO/IEC 27000:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/73906.html). In practice, it is threatened by eavesdropping on network traffic, data breaches resulting from compromised credentials or misconfigured systems, and insider threats where authorised users access or exfiltrate data beyond their permitted scope. Common controls include encryption of data at rest and in transit, access control mechanisms that enforce the principle of least privilege, and data classification policies that determine handling requirements based on sensitivity.
- **Integrity** is the property of accuracy and completeness of information, as defined in [ISO/IEC 27000:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/73906.html). It is threatened by unauthorised modification of data, whether through tampering, man-in-the-middle attacks that alter data in transit, or malware that corrupts files or system configurations. Controls used to protect integrity include cryptographic hashing functions such as SHA-256, which allow recipients to verify that data has not been altered, digital signatures that bind a message to a verified sender, and checksums used to detect transmission errors or file corruption.
- **Availability** is the property of being accessible and usable on demand by an authorised entity, as defined in [ISO/IEC 27000:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/73906.html). It is threatened by distributed denial-of-service attacks that overwhelm systems with traffic, ransomware that encrypts data and renders systems inoperable until a payment is made, and hardware failures that take services offline. Controls include redundant infrastructure, regular backups stored separately from primary systems, failover mechanisms that switch to backup systems automatically, and service level agreements that define acceptable downtime thresholds with service providers.

[ISO/IEC 27000:2018](https://www.iso.org/standard/73906.html) notes that information security may also involve additional properties beyond the three core ones. Authenticity refers to the verified identity of a user, system, or message, ensuring that an entity is what it claims to be. Non-repudiation is the ability to prove that an action or event has taken place, such that the responsible party cannot later deny it. Accountability is the property that actions of an entity can be traced back to that entity, typically through audit logs and identity management systems.

The three properties of the triad are not always compatible in practice. Stronger confidentiality controls, such as encryption and strict access restrictions, can reduce the speed and ease with which authorised users access data, affecting availability and usability. High availability requirements, such as replicating data across multiple systems, can increase the attack surface and introduce additional confidentiality risks. Security design therefore involves deliberate trade-offs, calibrated to the risk profile and operational requirements of the organisation.


### 1.2.2 Current Threat Landscape

Cybersecurity threats vary considerably in their technical sophistication, the actors behind them, and the assets they target. The following sections outline the main categories of threats, the actors who carry them out, and several trends that have shaped the threat environment in recent years.

#### Categories of threats

Malware is software designed to disrupt, damage, or gain unauthorised access to a system. It encompasses several distinct types: viruses attach themselves to legitimate files and spread when those files are executed; worms propagate across networks without requiring user interaction; trojans disguise themselves as legitimate software to gain access; ransomware encrypts files or systems and demands payment for their restoration; spyware collects information about a user or system without their knowledge; and adware delivers unwanted advertising, sometimes bundled with data collection functions.

![Categories of threats](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1threats.svg)

#### Threat actors and motivations

![Actors](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1actors.svg)

#### Recent trends

Ransomware-as-a-service has lowered the technical barrier to conducting ransomware attacks by allowing criminal groups to lease ransomware infrastructure and tooling to affiliates in exchange for a share of the proceeds. This model has contributed to a significant increase in the volume and variety of ransomware incidents.

Cloud environments have become an increasingly common target as organisations migrate infrastructure and data away from on-premises systems. Misconfigurations, weak access controls, and the complexity of shared responsibility models between providers and customers have been recurring factors in cloud-related incidents.

Artificial intelligence tools are being used by attackers to automate reconnaissance, generate more convincing phishing content, and identify vulnerabilities at scale. The same tools are also being adopted by defenders for threat detection and anomaly analysis.

Several incidents in recent years have illustrated the scale and complexity of modern attacks. The SolarWinds compromise, disclosed in December 2020, involved the insertion of malicious code into software updates distributed to thousands of organisations, including multiple United States federal agencies. The Log4Shell vulnerability, disclosed in December 2021, affected a widely used Java logging library and exposed a large number of internet-facing systems to remote code execution. The Colonial Pipeline ransomware attack in May 2021 disrupted fuel supply across the eastern United States and prompted a federal emergency declaration.

#### Attack surfaces

The range of assets exposed to potential attack has expanded as organisations adopt new technologies. Endpoints, including workstations, laptops, and mobile devices, remain a primary entry point. Cloud infrastructure introduces risks associated with misconfiguration and third-party access. Internet of Things devices, which often run minimal software with limited security controls, represent a growing and frequently underprotected category. Mobile devices are targeted through malicious applications, network interception, and operating system vulnerabilities. The human factor cuts across all of these: social engineering and credential-based attacks exploit human behaviour rather than technical weaknesses, and remain among the most consistently effective attack vectors.










## 1.3 Regulatory and Legal Frameworks

### 1.3.1 Applicable Regulations

Organisations that collect, process, or store data are subject to a range of legal and regulatory requirements depending on their sector, the nature of the data they handle, and the jurisdictions in which they operate. The following regulations represent the most widely applicable frameworks in force as of 2025.

#### General Data Protection Regulation

The [General Data Protection Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679) entered into force on 25 May 2018. It applies to any organisation that processes the personal data of individuals located in the European Union, regardless of where the organisation itself is established. It also applies to organisations established in the EU that process personal data, irrespective of where the data subjects are located.

**Scope and applicability**

The GDPR applies to the processing of personal data, defined in [Article 4(1)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e1489-1-1) as 

>any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. 

It covers both automated and manual processing where data is held in a structured filing system. Certain categories of data, including health data, biometric data, and data revealing racial or ethnic origin, are classified as special categories under [Article 9](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2051-1-1) and are subject to stricter conditions for processing.

Organisations established outside the EU are subject to the regulation when they offer goods or services to individuals in the EU, or when they monitor the behaviour of individuals within the EU, as set out in [Article 3(2)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e1374-1-1).

**Key principles**

[Article 5](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e1802-1-1) of the GDPR establishes six principles that govern all processing of personal data:

- **Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency**: data must be processed on a lawful basis and in a manner that is transparent to the data subject.
- **Purpose limitation**: data must be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not processed in a manner incompatible with those purposes.
- **Data minimisation**: data collected must be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary for the stated purpose.
- **Accuracy**: data must be kept accurate and, where necessary, up to date.
- **Storage limitation**: data must not be kept in a form that identifies individuals for longer than necessary.
- **Integrity and confidentiality**: data must be processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security, including protection against unauthorised access, accidental loss, or destruction.

**Rights of individuals**

[Chapter III](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2161-1-1) of the GDPR grants data subjects a set of enforceable rights:

- The right to be informed about how their data is used ([Articles 13–14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2254-1-1)).
- The right of access to their personal data ([Article 15](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2370-1-1)).
- The right to rectification of inaccurate data ([Article 16](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2399-1-1)).
- The right to erasure, under certain conditions ([Article 17](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2421-1-1)).
- The right to restriction of processing ([Article 18](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2465-1-1)).
- The right to data portability ([Article 20](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2510-1-1)).
- The right to object to processing, including for direct marketing purposes ([Article 21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2537-1-1)).
- Rights related to automated decision-making and profiling ([Article 22](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2564-1-1)).

**Obligations for organisations**

Controllers, defined in [Article 4(7)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e1489-1-1) as entities that determine the purposes and means of processing, must implement appropriate technical and organisational measures to ensure compliance. Key obligations include:

- Maintaining records of processing activities under [Article 30](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2807-1-1).
- Conducting Data Protection Impact Assessments for high-risk processing under [Article 35](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2927-1-1).
- Appointing a Data Protection Officer where required under [Article 37](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2981-1-1).
- Notifying the relevant supervisory authority of personal data breaches within 72 hours of becoming aware of them, under [Article 33](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2880-1-1).
- Notifying affected individuals without undue delay where a breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms, under [Article 34](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2907-1-1).

**Penalties for non-compliance**

[Article 83](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e3807-1-1) of the GDPR establishes a two-tier penalty structure. Infringements of the most fundamental obligations, including the principles of processing, the conditions for consent, and the rights of data subjects, can result in fines of up to 20 million euros or 4% of total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Less severe infringements, such as failures in record-keeping or notification obligations, carry fines of up to 10 million euros or 2% of worldwide annual turnover.

| | **Lower level** | **Higher level** |
|---|---|---|
| **Fine ceiling** | Up to €10 million | Up to €20 million |
| **Or, for companies** | Up to 2% of global annual turnover | Up to 4% of global annual turnover |
| **Which applies?** | Whichever of the two figures above is higher | Whichever of the two figures above is higher |
| **Example violations** | Failing to keep records of processing activities; not carrying out a data protection impact assessment; breaching data protection by design rules | Breaking core processing principles; violating people's data rights; transferring data outside the EU without safeguards; ignoring a data protection authority's order |

Where a company breaks several rules through the same actions, the total fine cannot exceed the ceiling that applies to the worst single violation.


#### NIS2 Directive

The [NIS2 Directive](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32022L2555) establishes a common baseline for cybersecurity across the European Union.

**Scope and sectors covered**

NIS2 applies to medium and large organisations operating in sectors designated as either essential or important. Essential sectors, listed in [Annex I](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32022L2555#anx_I) of the directive, include energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, wastewater, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, public administration, and space. Important sectors, listed in [Annex II](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32022L2555#anx_II), include postal and courier services, waste management, manufacture of certain critical products, food production and distribution, digital providers, and research organisations.

Member states may also apply the directive to smaller entities where they present a significant risk. Certain entities, such as providers of public electronic communications networks, are subject to the directive regardless of size.

**Security obligations**

[Article 21](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32022L2555#art_21) of NIS2 requires covered entities to implement appropriate and proportionate technical, operational, and organisational measures to manage cybersecurity risks. These measures must address at minimum:

- Risk analysis and information system security policies.
- Incident handling procedures.
- Business continuity and crisis management, including backup management and disaster recovery.
- Supply chain security, including the security of relationships with direct suppliers and service providers.
- Security in network and information systems acquisition, development, and maintenance, including vulnerability handling and disclosure.
- Policies and procedures to assess the effectiveness of cybersecurity risk management measures.
- Basic cyber hygiene practices and cybersecurity training.
- Policies and procedures relating to the use of cryptography and, where appropriate, encryption.
- Human resources security, access control policies, and asset management.
- The use of multi-factor authentication or continuous authentication solutions.

**Incident reporting requirements**

[Article 23](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32022L2555#art_23) of NIS2 establishes a multi-stage reporting obligation for significant incidents. Entities must submit an early warning to the relevant national authority or [Computer Security Incident Response Team](https://www.enisa.europa.eu/topics/csirts-in-europe) within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident. A more detailed incident notification must follow within 72 hours. A final report is required no later than one month after the incident notification.

An incident is considered significant if it has caused or is capable of causing severe operational disruption, financial loss, or material or immaterial damage to other natural or legal persons.

#### National regulations

Beyond the major supranational frameworks, several national-level instruments are relevant to organisations operating in specific jurisdictions.

In France, the [Loi de Programmation Militaire](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/JORFTEXT000028338825) introduced obligations for operators of vital importance to implement security measures and report incidents to the [Agence Nationale de la Sécurité des Systèmes d'Information](https://www.ssi.gouv.fr/). The ANSSI publishes technical guidelines and reference frameworks, including the [Référentiel Général de Sécurité](https://www.ssi.gouv.fr/entreprise/reglementation/confiance-numerique/le-referentiel-general-de-securite-rgs/), which applies to public administrations and their digital services.

In the United Kingdom, the [Cyber Essentials](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/cyberessentials/overview) scheme, administered by the [National Cyber Security Centre](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/), defines a baseline set of technical controls that organisations can certify against. The scheme has two levels: Cyber Essentials, which involves a self-assessment, and Cyber Essentials Plus, which requires independent technical verification. Certification is mandatory for suppliers bidding for certain UK government contracts involving the handling of sensitive information.

### 1.3.2 Technical Reference Standards

Several internationally recognised frameworks provide structured guidance for implementing and assessing information security controls. Organisations typically adopt one or more of these frameworks depending on their sector, regulatory environment, and operational maturity.

#### ISO/IEC 27001

[ISO/IEC 27001](https://www.iso.org/standard/27001) is the international standard for Information Security Management Systems. It specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving a documented management system that governs how an organisation identifies and addresses information security risks.

The certification process involves three stages. First, the organisation implements the management system and conducts an internal audit. Second, an accredited certification body performs a Stage 1 audit to review documentation, followed by a Stage 2 audit to assess whether the system is operating effectively. If both audits are passed, the organisation receives certification, which is valid for three years and subject to annual surveillance audits.

Annex A of the standard lists 93 controls organised into four themes: organisational controls, people controls, physical controls, and technological controls. These controls are not all mandatory; organisations select and apply those relevant to their risk assessment, documenting their choices in a Statement of Applicability.

![Network Topology](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1ISO27001.png)


**ISO/IEC 27002**

[ISO/IEC 27002](https://www.iso.org/standard/75652.html) is a companion document to ISO/IEC 27001. Where ISO/IEC 27001 defines the requirements for a management system, ISO/IEC 27002 provides detailed implementation guidance for each of the controls listed in Annex A. It describes the purpose of each control, the rationale for its use, and practical guidance on how to implement it. Organisations use ISO/IEC 27002 as a reference when selecting and applying controls, but it is not itself a certifiable standard.

#### NIST Cybersecurity Framework

The [NIST Cybersecurity Framework](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework), currently at version 2.0 published in February 2024, organises cybersecurity activity around six core functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover. Each function is subdivided into categories and subcategories that describe specific outcomes, such as maintaining an asset inventory, implementing access controls, or establishing incident response procedures.

![Network Topology](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1NIST.svg)

The framework also defines four implementation tiers, ranging from Tier 1 (Partial) to Tier 4 (Adaptive), which describe the degree to which an organisation's cybersecurity risk management practices are formalised, risk-informed, and integrated into broader organisational processes. Tiers are not intended as maturity levels to be maximised; they are reference points for assessing current practice against organisational goals.

Profiles allow organisations to map the framework's outcomes to their specific business requirements, risk tolerance, and available resources. A current profile describes the outcomes an organisation is already achieving; a target profile describes the outcomes it intends to achieve. The gap between the two informs prioritisation decisions.

#### NIST SP 800-53

[NIST SP 800-53](https://csrc.nist.gov/publications/detail/sp/800-53/rev-5/final), currently at Revision 5, provides a catalogue of security and privacy controls for federal information systems and organisations in the United States. It is referenced by the Federal Information Security Modernization Act and is used as the basis for system authorisation under the Risk Management Framework.

The catalogue organises controls into 20 families, covering areas such as access control, audit and accountability, configuration management, incident response, and supply chain risk management. Each control includes a description, supplemental guidance, and control enhancements for higher-impact systems. Although the standard was developed for federal use, it is widely adopted in the private sector, particularly by organisations that contract with US government agencies.

![Network Topology](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/120families.svg)


#### CIS Controls

The [CIS Controls](https://www.cisecurity.org/controls), published by the Center for Internet Security and currently at version 8, comprise 18 control categories covering the most common attack vectors and defensive measures. Examples include inventory and control of enterprise assets, data protection, secure configuration of enterprise assets and software, and audit log management.

The controls are organised into three implementation groups. Implementation Group 1 covers a basic set of controls suitable for organisations with limited cybersecurity resources and a lower risk profile. Implementation Group 2 adds controls appropriate for organisations with moderate resources and greater exposure. Implementation Group 3 encompasses the full set of controls and is intended for organisations with significant cybersecurity expertise and high-risk environments. This tiered structure allows organisations to prioritise implementation based on their capacity and risk context.

![Network Topology](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1implementations.svg)

#### SOC 2

[SOC 2](https://www.aicpa-cima.com/resources/landing/system-and-organization-controls-soc-suite-of-services) is an auditing framework developed by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants. It applies to service organisations that store, process, or transmit customer data, and assesses controls against five trust service criteria: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy. The security criterion is required in all SOC 2 engagements; the remaining four are included at the discretion of the organisation based on the services it provides.

SOC 2 reports are produced in two forms. A Type I report assesses whether the controls described by the organisation are suitably designed at a specific point in time. A Type II report covers a defined period, typically six to twelve months, and assesses whether those controls operated effectively throughout that period. Type II reports are generally considered more meaningful by customers and auditors because they reflect sustained operational performance rather than a snapshot.


### 1.3.3 Key Roles in Information Security
Information security functions are distributed across several distinct roles, each with a defined scope of responsibility. In larger organisations, these roles are occupied by dedicated individuals or teams; in smaller ones, a single person may cover several of them. The following sections describe each role, its responsibilities, and the certifications commonly associated with it.

#### Chief Information Security Officer

The Chief Information Security Officer is the senior executive responsible for an organisation's information security programme. The role is strategic : they define the security strategy, set risk tolerance in alignment with business objectives, oversee the security budget, and ensure that the organisation meets its regulatory and contractual obligations.

Day-to-day responsibilities include commissioning risk assessments, reviewing audit findings, communicating the security posture to executive leadership and the board, and coordinating the organisation's response to major incidents. The Chief Information Security Officer also represents the organisation in dealings with regulators and, where relevant, with law enforcement.

Certifications commonly associated with this role include [CISSP](https://www.isc2.org/certifications/cissp) (Certified Information Systems Security Professional), [CISM](https://www.isaca.org/credentialing/cism) (Certified Information Security Manager), and [CRISC](https://www.isaca.org/credentialing/crisc) (Certified in Risk and Information Systems Control).

#### Data Protection Officer

The Data Protection Officer is a role defined in [Article 37 of the GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e2981-1-1). Appointment is mandatory for public authorities, for organisations that carry out large-scale systematic monitoring of individuals, and for organisations that process special categories of data on a large scale.

The Data Protection Officer's tasks are set out in [Article 39](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e3031-1-1) and include informing and advising the organisation on its obligations under data protection law, monitoring compliance with the regulation, advising on Data Protection Impact Assessments, and acting as the contact point for the supervisory authority.

A key requirement is independence: [Article 38](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679#d1e3006-1-1) states that the Data Protection Officer must not receive instructions regarding the exercise of their tasks and must not be dismissed or penalised for performing them.

Relevant certifications include the [CIPP/E](https://iapp.org/certify/cipp/) (Certified Information Privacy Professional / Europe) and [CIPM](https://iapp.org/certify/cipm/) (Certified Information Privacy Manager), both issued by the International Association of Privacy Professionals.

#### Risk Manager and Governance, Risk, and Compliance Analyst

The risk manager and Governance, Risk, and Compliance analyst are responsible for the structured identification, assessment, and treatment of information security risks, and for ensuring that the organisation's security practices align with applicable regulations, standards, and internal policies.

Risk management in this context follows a defined process: identifying assets and threats, assessing the likelihood and potential impact of adverse events, selecting appropriate treatment options (acceptance, mitigation, transfer, or avoidance), and tracking residual risk over time. This work is typically documented in a risk register and reviewed on a regular cycle.

The Governance, Risk, and Compliance function extends this to include regulatory compliance monitoring, policy development, internal audit coordination, and reporting to senior management and the board. In organisations subject to multiple regulatory frameworks, such as the GDPR, NIS2, and ISO/IEC 27001 simultaneously, the Governance, Risk, and Compliance analyst maps controls across frameworks to avoid duplication and identify gaps.

Certifications associated with these roles include [CRISC](https://www.isaca.org/credentialing/crisc) (Certified in Risk and Information Systems Control), [CISM](https://www.isaca.org/credentialing/cism), [ISO 27001 Lead Implementer](https://pecb.com/en/education-and-certification-for-individuals/iso-iec-27001/iso-iec-27001-lead-implementer), and [ISO 27001 Lead Auditor](https://pecb.com/en/education-and-certification-for-individuals/iso-iec-27001/iso-iec-27001-lead-auditor).

#### Security Architect

A security architect designs the security controls and structures that protect an organisation's systems and infrastructure. The role operates at the intersection of technical depth and strategic planning: the security architect must understand both the threat landscape and the organisation's business requirements, and translate that understanding into a coherent set of design decisions.

Responsibilities include defining security reference architectures, reviewing proposed system designs for security weaknesses, selecting and specifying security technologies, and establishing standards for areas such as network segmentation, identity and access management, encryption, and cloud security. The security architect also contributes to the organisation's risk management process by assessing the security implications of architectural choices.

In practice, the role requires close collaboration with infrastructure teams, application developers, and the Chief Information Security Officer, as security requirements must be embedded into systems from the design stage rather than added after deployment.

Certifications associated with this role include [CISSP-ISSAP](https://www.isc2.org/certifications/cissp-concentrations) (Information Systems Security Architecture Professional), [SABSA](https://sabsa.org/certification/) (Sherwood Applied Business Security Architecture), and [TOGAF](https://www.opengroup.org/togaf) for those working within broader enterprise architecture frameworks.


#### Security Operations Center Analyst

Security Operations Center analysts monitor an organisation's systems and networks for security events, investigate alerts, and coordinate responses to confirmed incidents. The role is typically structured into three tiers reflecting increasing levels of expertise and responsibility.

- A Level 1 analyst performs continuous monitoring of security tools. Their primary function is triage: reviewing alerts, filtering false positives, and escalating events that require further investigation.

- A Level 2 analyst conducts deeper investigation of escalated alerts. This involves correlating events across multiple data sources, analysing indicators of compromise, and determining the scope and nature of a potential incident. They also develop and refine detection rules based on observed attack patterns.

- A Level 3 analyst handles the most complex incidents and performs proactive threat hunting, searching for evidence of adversary activity that has not triggered automated alerts. They may also conduct forensic analysis, contribute to threat intelligence, and advise on improvements to the detection and response capability.

Certifications associated with this role include [CompTIA Security+](https://www.comptia.org/certifications/security), [CompTIA CySA+](https://www.comptia.org/certifications/cybersecurity-analyst), [GIAC Security Essentials](https://www.giac.org/certifications/security-essentials-gsec/) and, at more senior levels, [GIAC Certified Incident Handler](https://www.giac.org/certifications/certified-incident-handler-gcih/).

#### Incident Responder

An incident responder manages the lifecycle of a security incident from detection through to resolution and post-incident review. The role requires both technical capability and the ability to coordinate across teams under time pressure.

During an incident, the responder's immediate priorities are containment, to prevent further damage or spread, and evidence preservation, to support forensic analysis and potential legal proceedings. This is followed by eradication of the threat, which may involve removing malware, revoking compromised credentials, or isolating affected systems. Once the threat is removed, the responder oversees the restoration of affected services and verifies that the environment is clean before returning systems to production.

After the incident, the responder contributes to a post-incident review that documents the timeline of events, the actions taken, and the lessons identified. This review informs updates to detection rules, response playbooks, and security controls.

Relevant certifications include [GCIH](https://www.giac.org/certifications/certified-incident-handler-gcih/) (GIAC Certified Incident Handler), [GCFE](https://www.giac.org/certifications/certified-forensic-examiner-gcfe/) (GIAC Certified Forensic Examiner), and [GCFA](https://www.giac.org/certifications/certified-forensic-analyst-gcfa/) (GIAC Certified Forensic Analyst).

#### Penetration Tester

A penetration tester, sometimes referred to as an ethical hacker, is engaged to simulate attacks against an organisation's systems, applications, or infrastructure in order to identify exploitable vulnerabilities before a malicious actor does. The work is conducted under a formal agreement that defines the scope, the permitted techniques, the timeframe, and the rules of engagement.

Engagements are typically categorised by the level of prior knowledge given to the tester. In a black-box assessment, the tester begins with no information about the target, simulating an external attacker. In a white-box assessment, the tester is given full access to documentation, source code, and architecture diagrams. Grey-box assessments fall between the two, providing partial information such as user-level credentials.

Methodologies used in penetration testing are documented in frameworks such as the [OWASP Testing Guide](https://owasp.org/www-project-web-security-testing-guide/), the [PTES](http://www.pentest-standard.org/index.php/Main_Page) (Penetration Testing Execution Standard), and [OSSTMM](https://www.isecom.org/OSSTMM.3.pdf) (Open Source Security Testing Methodology Manual). Findings are documented in a report that describes each vulnerability, its potential impact, and recommended remediation steps.

Certifications associated with this role include [OSCP](https://www.offsec.com/courses/pen-200/) (Offensive Security Certified Professional), [CEH](https://www.eccouncil.org/train-certify/certified-ethical-hacker-ceh/) (Certified Ethical Hacker), and [GPEN](https://www.giac.org/certifications/penetration-tester-gpen/) (GIAC Penetration Tester).

## 1.4 Threat Models and Analysis Frameworks

### 1.4.1 MITRE ATT&CK
[MITRE ATT&CK](https://attack.mitre.org/) is a publicly available knowledge base maintained by the MITRE Corporation that catalogues the tactics, techniques, and procedures observed in real-world attacks. The framework is organised into three levels. 
- Tactics represent the adversary's objective at a given stage of an attack, such as gaining initial access, moving laterally, or exfiltrating data. Fourteen tactics are defined in the Enterprise matrix. 
- Techniques describe how that objective is achieved, and sub-techniques provide a more granular breakdown where relevant. 
- Procedures are documented instances of a specific technique being used by a named threat actor or malware family.

Three matrices are available: Enterprise, Mobile, and ICS, each covering a distinct environment.

In practice, the framework is used in several ways. Security teams map threat intelligence reports to ATT&CK techniques to understand which adversary behaviours are relevant to their environment. Detection engineers use it to identify gaps in monitoring coverage and to write or refine detection rules. Red and blue team exercises are structured around ATT&CK tactics to ensure that both offensive scenarios and defensive responses reflect documented adversary behaviour. The ATT&CK Navigator, a browser-based tool, allows teams to visualise which techniques are covered by existing controls and where gaps remain.

However, the framework also describes what adversaries do, not what defenders should do, so it does not prescribe specific controls or remediation steps.

### 1.4.2 Cyber Kill Chain
The Cyber Kill Chain is a model developed by Lockheed Martin, in a paper titled "[Intelligence-Driven Computer Network Defense Informed by Analysis of Adversary Campaigns and Intrusion Kill Chains](https://securityandtechnology.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/lm-white-paper-intel-driven-defense.pdf)". It describes an intrusion as a sequence of seven phases, and proposes that disrupting any one phase is sufficient to stop the attack.

![Cyber Kill Chain](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1CyberKillChain.svg)

The seven phases are reconnaissance, where the attacker gathers information about the target; weaponisation, where a malicious payload is created; delivery, where the payload is transmitted to the target; exploitation, where a vulnerability is triggered; installation, where malware or a backdoor is established on the system; command and control, where the attacker establishes remote access; and actions on objectives, where the attacker carries out their ultimate goal, such as data exfiltration, destruction, or fraud.

The model's primary use is defensive prioritisation. By identifying at which phase an intrusion was detected, a security team can assess how far an attacker progressed and where controls failed. It also provides a common language for structuring threat intelligence and incident reports.

The model has several limitations. It was designed with external, malware-based intrusions in mind and is less applicable to insider threats or attacks that do not follow a linear progression. It also gives limited coverage to post-compromise activity such as lateral movement and privilege escalation, which MITRE ATT&CK addresses in considerably more detail. The two frameworks are often used together: the Kill Chain provides a high-level view of the attack lifecycle, while ATT&CK supplies the granular technique-level detail needed for detection and response.

### 1.4.3 STRIDE
[STRIDE](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/security/develop/threat-modeling-tool-threats) is a threat modelling framework developed by Microsoft. It provides a structured way to identify security threats during the design phase of a system, before implementation begins. The name is an acronym formed from the six threat categories it defines.

![STRIDE](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1STRIDE.svg)

Each category maps to one or more of the classic security properties: spoofing violates authentication, tampering violates integrity, repudiation violates non-repudiation, information disclosure violates confidentiality, denial of service violates availability, and elevation of privilege violates authorisation.

In practice, STRIDE is applied to a Data Flow Diagram of the system under review. The diagram is divided into components, such as processes, data stores, external entities, and data flows, and trust boundaries are drawn where data crosses a security perimeter. Each component is then examined against the six categories to enumerate plausible threats. This process is typically carried out during architecture review or as part of a secure development lifecycle.

Microsoft provides a dedicated Threat Modeling Tool that automates parts of this process, generating a list of potential STRIDE threats from a diagram drawn within the tool.

### 1.4.4 OWASP
[OWASP](https://owasp.org/), the Open Web Application Security Project, is a nonprofit foundation that produces freely available documentation, tools, and standards for web application security. It operates through volunteer-led projects and is governed as a community organisation with no commercial affiliation.

![OSWAP](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/1illustrations/1OSWAP.png)

Its most widely referenced output is the OWASP Top 10, a ranked list of the most critical web application security risks, updated periodically based on data collected from security firms and bug bounty programmes. The current edition, published in 2021, lists the following categories.

Broken access control occurs when restrictions on what authenticated users are permitted to do are not properly enforced, allowing attackers to access unauthorised functionality or data. Cryptographic failures refer to weaknesses in the protection of data in transit or at rest, such as the use of outdated algorithms or the absence of encryption where it is required. Injection covers attacks in which untrusted data is sent to an interpreter as part of a command or query, including SQL, NoSQL, and LDAP injection. Insecure design refers to architectural and design flaws that cannot be remediated by correct implementation alone, reflecting an absence of security considerations during the design phase. Security misconfiguration encompasses insecure default settings, unnecessary features left enabled, and improperly configured permissions. Vulnerable and outdated components refers to the use of libraries, frameworks, or other software with known vulnerabilities. Identification and authentication failures cover weaknesses in session management, credential handling, and identity verification. Software and data integrity failures refer to code and infrastructure that does not protect against unauthorised modification, including insecure deserialisation and compromised update mechanisms. Security logging and monitoring failures describe the absence of sufficient logging, alerting, and response capability to detect and respond to breaches. Server-side request forgery occurs when a web application fetches a remote resource based on user-supplied input without adequate validation, allowing an attacker to cause the server to make requests to unintended destinations.

Beyond the Top 10, OWASP maintains several other resources. The OWASP Testing Guide provides a methodology for conducting web application security assessments. The Application Security Verification Standard defines security requirements for web applications at three levels of assurance, and is used as a basis for security audits and procurement requirements. The Software Assurance Maturity Model provides a framework for evaluating and improving a software development organisation's security practices. The Cheat Sheet Series offers concise, developer-facing guidance on specific security topics such as input validation, authentication, and cryptographic storage.

In practice, OWASP resources are used across different stages of the software lifecycle. Developers use the Cheat Sheet Series and Top 10 during implementation. Security teams use the Testing Guide during audits. The Application Security Verification Standard is used to define contractual security requirements. In relation to other frameworks, the OWASP Top 10 categories map to specific techniques in MITRE ATT&CK, and the threats they describe can be identified during a STRIDE analysis at the design stage. The three frameworks address different levels of abstraction and are commonly used together rather than as alternatives.
