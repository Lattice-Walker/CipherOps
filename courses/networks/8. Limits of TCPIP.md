#  Limits of TCP/IP and Alternative Architectures

## 1 Pillars and Conceptual Flaws of TCP/IP

TCP/IP was developed in the early 1970s by Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn as part of the DARPA-funded ARPANET project. The foundational specification was published as RFC 675 in 1974 and was later refined into the separate Internet Protocol and Transmission Control Protocol specifications published as RFC 791 and RFC 793 in 1981.

The protocol was designed to support communication across heterogeneous and unreliable networks, with an explicit design goal of surviving partial network failures, including the destruction of individual nodes. This objective reflected the military and research context in which the ARPANET was developed.

A central design concept underlying this architecture is the end-to-end principle, articulated by Saltzer, Reed, and Clark in a 1984 paper. According to this principle, intelligence and complex functionality should be placed at the endpoints of a communication rather than within the network itself, which should remain simple and general-purpose.

Because the protocol stack does not depend on any particular physical medium, TCP/IP can run over Ethernet, radio, satellite, and optical links without modification to the core protocols. This independence from the underlying medium allowed the architecture to scale from a small number of research nodes to billions of connected devices without requiring a fundamental redesign.

The specification was published as an open, royalty-free standard, which allowed any vendor or developer to implement compatible systems and contributed to the emergence of a global, multi-vendor ecosystem. The layered structure of the protocol stack, separating the link, network, transport, and application layers, allowed each layer to evolve independently of the others. The absence of a single point of control also gave the architecture a degree of resilience, since routing can adapt dynamically to changes in network topology without requiring centralised coordination.

### 1.1 Critical Design Decisions

The Internet Protocol provides best-effort delivery: the network makes no guarantee regarding whether a packet will be delivered, in what order, or within what time frame. Responsibility for reliability is delegated to the transport layer or to the application itself.

Routers in this architecture perform stateless routing, forwarding each packet independently based on its destination address without maintaining state associated with individual flows. This design simplifies router implementation but limits the extent to which traffic can be engineered at the network layer.

A further structural characteristic is that an IP address simultaneously identifies a host and describes its topological location within the network. This conflation of identity and location creates difficulties for mobility, since a device that changes its point of attachment must generally also change its address, and for multihoming, where a single host or network seeks reachability through multiple providers.

Although extension mechanisms exist, the core header formats defined in the 1980s remain in use today. Any change to these formats requires coordinated deployment across a large number of independently operated networks, which has historically proceeded slowly.

The original design assumed a trusted community of researchers and did not include a mechanism to verify the origin of a packet or the identity of communicating parties. The original classful addressing scheme, which allocated address space in fixed-size blocks, was replaced by Classless Inter-Domain Routing in 1993. The transition nonetheless left lasting complexity in routing and address management practices that persists in current allocations.

Jon Postel's robustness principle, generally summarised as "be conservative in what you send, be liberal in what you accept," encouraged interoperability between independently developed implementations. According to later analyses, this same principle also contributed to ambiguous protocol implementations and associated security vulnerabilities, since liberal acceptance of malformed input can be exploited by an attacker.

Critical resources within this architecture, including IP address allocation, Autonomous System Number assignment, and management of the DNS root zone, are coordinated by the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority and the regional internet registries. This coordination introduces organisational dependencies on a small number of governing bodies.

### 1.2 Structural Limitations

The global BGP routing table exceeded 900,000 IPv4 prefixes by the mid-2020s, and the memory and processing requirements for routers maintaining a full routing table have continued to increase as a result.

IP addresses are topologically bound to a network, so a device that moves between networks generally receives a new address, which breaks any existing connections associated with the previous address. Mobile IP and Multipath TCP address this limitation partially, but neither is universally deployed.

Connecting a host or network to multiple upstream providers simultaneously, a practice known as multihoming, requires either direct participation in BGP or the use of Network Address Translation-based workarounds, both of which add operational complexity compared with a single-homed connection.

Middleboxes, including firewalls, Network Address Translation devices, and load balancers, have become sufficiently prevalent that they enforce implicit assumptions about packet formats and protocol behaviour. This makes it difficult to deploy new transport protocols across the existing internet, a phenomenon generally referred to as protocol ossification. QUIC, a transport protocol built on top of UDP, was developed in part to address this problem by avoiding the assumptions that middleboxes make about TCP traffic specifically.

TCP congestion control was added by Van Jacobson in 1988, several years after the protocol had already been deployed at scale. The network experienced congestion collapse before a solution to this problem existed, meaning congestion control was introduced as a corrective measure rather than as part of the original design.

The architecture provides no inherent isolation between users or applications sharing a bottleneck link, meaning a single high-bandwidth flow can affect the performance experienced by all other flows traversing the same link.

The Domain Name System relies on a hierarchical structure with a small number of root servers and a governance model that concentrates administrative control. This centralised structure also makes the Domain Name System a common point of control for censorship and surveillance, since queries and responses can be inspected, blocked, or redirected at the resolver or network level.

The Border Gateway Protocol has no built-in mechanism to verify that an autonomous system is authorised to announce a given address prefix. Route hijacking and route leaks remain operational risks despite the deployment of Resource Public Key Infrastructure, which addresses only part of this underlying trust problem.

## 2 Structural Security Problems in TCP/IP

### 2.1 Absence of Native Encryption

The ARPANET originally connected a small number of trusted institutions, and confidentiality was not treated as a design requirement for the resulting protocol suite. Core application protocols developed on top of TCP/IP, including HTTP, SMTP, FTP, DNS, and Telnet, transmit data without encryption by default.

Encryption was subsequently retrofitted through wrappers or replacement protocols rather than being incorporated at the network layer: Transport Layer Security added to HTTP, the STARTTLS extension added to SMTP, and SSH developed as a replacement for Telnet. Because this approach applies encryption on a protocol-by-protocol basis, its deployment is inconsistent across applications.

IPsec provides encryption at the network layer but requires explicit configuration on both communicating endpoints and is not universally deployed between arbitrary hosts on the internet.

The absence of default encryption made large-scale passive interception of internet traffic technically straightforward. The disclosures made by Edward Snowden in 2013 documented systematic exploitation of this property by intelligence agencies.

DNS queries are transmitted in plaintext by default, which can reveal a user's browsing behaviour to network operators or to any party capable of observing traffic along the path. DNS over TLS and DNS over HTTPS address this specific exposure but are not universally deployed.

Even where payload encryption is applied, metadata such as source and destination addresses, timing, and traffic volume remains visible to a network observer. Encryption of payload content therefore does not, on its own, provide complete communication privacy.

The security of Transport Layer Security depends on the certificate authority system, which has experienced mis-issuance incidents in the past and which operates under legal frameworks that, in various jurisdictions, permit government authorities to compel certificate authorities to act in specific ways.

### 2.2 Lack of Sovereignty and Dependencies

Management of the DNS root zone is carried out under a structure historically tied to United States government oversight. ICANN coordinates policy for this structure but operates within a governance framework that reflects its institutional origin.

Regional internet registries allocate IP address space under the coordination of the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority. An organisation that loses its registry membership, or that becomes subject to sanctions, may lose access to its existing address allocations as a consequence.

Any autonomous system can technically announce any address prefix under the Border Gateway Protocol, which relies on operational norms and route filtering rather than cryptographic verification to prevent unauthorised announcements. This trust model has been exploited by state-level actors in documented cases of route hijacking.

Internet traffic exchanged between two points within the same country may nonetheless transit networks located in other countries, as a result of routing economics rather than geographic necessity, exposing that traffic to the legal jurisdiction of the countries it transits.

A substantial share of global internet infrastructure, including root DNS servers, major content delivery networks, cloud computing platforms, and certificate authorities, is operated by entities based in the United States and therefore subject to United States law.

Some states have implemented or proposed national internet infrastructure intended to allow disconnection from the global internet if required. Examples include Russia's Sovereign Internet Law and the existing architecture of internet infrastructure in China.

Intercontinental internet connectivity depends on a relatively small number of submarine cable systems. Physical damage to these cables, or legal interception carried out at their landing stations, represents a structural vulnerability for the networks that depend on them.

Laws such as the United States CLOUD Act allow authorities to compel the disclosure of data held by United States-based providers regardless of the physical location in which that data is stored.

### 2.3 Unanticipated New Requirements

The original TCP/IP protocol stack was designed for general-purpose computers. Constrained devices used in Internet of Things deployments, which operate with limited memory, processing power, and energy budgets, require adapted protocols such as CoAP, 6LoWPAN, and MQTT.

TCP's reliability mechanisms, including retransmission and in-order delivery, introduce latency and jitter that are incompatible with the requirements of real-time audio and video communication. UDP-based protocols and, more recently, QUIC were developed in response to this constraint.

TCP/IP routes packets to addresses rather than to named content. As demand has grown for retrieving specific content regardless of its location, this mismatch has contributed to the complexity of content delivery network architectures and has motivated research into alternative approaches such as Named Data Networking.

TCP/IP assumes continuous end-to-end connectivity between communicating hosts. Environments such as deep space communication, disaster response, and remote rural areas without continuous connectivity require store-and-forward architectures, generally referred to as delay-tolerant networking, which the standard protocol stack does not natively support.

The cryptographic algorithms currently underpinning TLS and IPsec are considered vulnerable to attacks by sufficiently capable quantum computers. Migration to post-quantum cryptographic algorithms requires coordinated updates across the entire protocol stack, rather than a change confined to a single layer or implementation.

The original architecture exposes source and destination addresses in every packet transmitted. Achieving meaningful communication privacy beyond this baseline requires additional layers, such as Tor, virtual private networks, or mixnets, none of which were part of the original protocol design.

The same properties that give TCP/IP its resilience, namely decentralisation and the absence of mandatory identity verification, also make attribution of malicious traffic difficult. This creates a tension between privacy and accountability that has no resolution within the existing architecture itself.

## 3 Reticulum

### 3.1 Overview and Motivations

Reticulum is an open-source network stack designed for encrypted communication over low-bandwidth, high-latency, and physically diverse links. It was created by Mark Qvist and is maintained as free software under the MIT licence.

According to the project's stated motivation, Reticulum is intended to provide secure communication in environments where conventional internet infrastructure is unavailable, unreliable, or considered untrustworthy. Cited use cases include off-grid communication, disaster response, communication in remote areas without cellular or internet coverage, and contexts requiring resistance to surveillance.

The design of Reticulum treats every node as equal: the architecture does not include servers, central authorities, or a registration requirement, and the network is described as functional with any number of participating nodes, including as few as two.

Reticulum is not built on top of the Internet Protocol. It operates as an independent network layer that can use IP-based links as one of several possible physical transports, rather than depending on IP as its underlying addressing or routing system. The protocol is documented at reticulum.network in sufficient detail to permit independent implementation.

### 3.2 Cryptographic Addressing

Every Reticulum identity consists of a cryptographic key pair. A node's network address is derived from its public key by truncating a hash of that key to 16 bytes.

Because the address is derived directly from the key pair, it is self-certifying: possession of the corresponding private key demonstrates ownership of the address without requiring verification by any external authority. There is no registry equivalent to the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority within Reticulum, and no mechanism by which a third party can revoke an address.

The 128-bit address space, derived from cryptographic hashes, makes accidental or deliberate address collision computationally infeasible using current algorithms. Because an identity is not tied to a physical network location, it can be used across any physical medium or network topology without reconfiguration.

This contrasts with IP addressing, in which an address is assigned by an administrative authority and describes a host's topological location. A Reticulum address is instead self-generated and conveys identity only, independent of the host's position within the network.

### 3.3 Decentralised Routing

Reticulum uses a destination-oriented routing model, in which nodes announce reachability for specific destinations rather than advertising address prefixes as in BGP.

When a node needs to communicate with a given destination, it broadcasts a path request. Any node that already has a known route to that destination responds with a path announcement, which the requesting node uses to establish communication.

Nodes maintain only the routes they have discovered and used, rather than a complete routing table equivalent to a BGP full table. Intermediate nodes that have enabled transport mode forward path requests and announcements and relay encrypted packets toward their destination on behalf of other nodes. Destination announcements propagate through the network with a decreasing hop count, which limits their reach and prevents unbounded flooding.

If an established path becomes unavailable, the affected node initiates a new path discovery process, allowing the network to adapt to topology changes without centralised coordination. According to the project's documentation, this routing model is suited to sparse, low-bandwidth networks; it has not been tested at internet scale and is not designed for that purpose.

### 3.4 Encryption by Default

All communication carried over Reticulum is encrypted, and the protocol does not define a mode of operation in which application data is transmitted in plaintext.

Reticulum uses X25519 for key exchange, Ed25519 for digital signatures, and AES-128 in CBC mode combined with HMAC-SHA256 for symmetric encryption, according to the current version of the specification. Each session uses an ephemeral key exchange, providing forward secrecy: compromise of a node's long-term private key does not expose the content of past sessions.

Encryption and authentication are applied at the level of individual packets. An observer of the network can determine packet size and timing but cannot determine packet content, and cannot determine the identities of the communicating parties when Reticulum's anonymous link feature is used.

Trust between parties is established through direct exchange of public keys or through out-of-band verification, rather than through a public key infrastructure hierarchy that could be compromised or subject to legal compulsion.

This differs from TLS, which encrypts the content of a transport session but exposes certain metadata, including the destination server's identity through the Server Name Indication field used during the handshake and through the certificate presented by the server. According to the project's documentation, Reticulum's design reduces this category of metadata exposure at the network layer.

### 3.5 Interoperability and Physical Media

Reticulum defines a generic interface layer, allowing any medium capable of transmitting and receiving data to be used as a transport for the protocol.

Supported interface types include TCP/IP sockets, used to tunnel Reticulum traffic over existing internet infrastructure, UDP, serial interfaces, LoRa radio modules, packet radio using the AX.25 protocol, I2P, and custom hardware interfaces.

LoRa radio modules operate in licence-exempt frequency bands and allow Reticulum nodes to communicate over distances of several kilometres without supporting infrastructure, at a bandwidth limited to a few kilobits per second. A single Reticulum network can span multiple physical media simultaneously, so that a single packet may travel over a LoRa link, then a serial connection, then a TCP tunnel, with routing between these media handled transparently by the protocol.

RNode is an open-hardware LoRa interface designed specifically for use with Reticulum, documented and buildable from publicly available schematics.

Because Reticulum is designed for low-bandwidth environments, it is not suited to high-throughput applications such as video streaming, and its protocol overhead is optimised for small packet sizes rather than sustained high-volume transfer.

### 3.6 Reticulum Communication Diagram

A diagram illustrating communication over Reticulum would typically show two or more end nodes, each identified by a cryptographic key pair and its derived address, along with one or more transport nodes acting as relays between end nodes that cannot reach each other directly. It would depict the path discovery phase, in which a path request originates at the initiating node and propagates through transport nodes until it reaches the destination, followed by a path announcement returned by the destination or by an intermediate node that already holds a known route. Once a path is established, the diagram would show encrypted packets flowing along it, with each hop forwarding traffic based on the address of the next transport node. Such a diagram would also typically illustrate a single node using multiple physical interface types simultaneously, reflecting support for heterogeneous media, and would not include any central server or authority, consistent with the fully decentralised structure of the architecture.

### 3.7 Current Limitations and Challenges

Reticulum is functional and under active development, but it has not undergone the extent of real-world deployment and adversarial testing that TCP/IP has accumulated over several decades of operation.

The protocol is explicitly designed for low-bandwidth links and is not intended as a replacement for TCP/IP in high-bandwidth environments. In sparse or large networks, path discovery may take a significant amount of time, which limits its suitability for latency-sensitive applications.

The application ecosystem built on Reticulum remains small. Primary applications include LXMF, a message format and delivery protocol, Nomad Network, a text-based communication platform, and Sideband, a messaging application. General-purpose internet applications do not run over Reticulum without modification.

Use of radio interfaces such as LoRa and packet radio is subject to national frequency regulations, and licence-exempt bands are generally subject to power and duty-cycle restrictions that limit achievable range and throughput.

The cryptographic primitives used by Reticulum are considered well established, but the protocol as a whole has not undergone formal verification, and migration to post-quantum cryptographic algorithms has not yet been addressed in the specification.

Communication using Reticulum requires that both parties run Reticulum-compatible software. There is no interoperability with conventional TCP/IP applications unless an explicit gateway is deployed between the two networks.

## 4 Resilient Peer-to-Peer Architectures

A peer-to-peer network is an architecture in which participants communicate directly with one another without relying on dedicated servers, and in which each node can act as both a consumer and a provider of resources. This contrasts with a client-server architecture, in which a small number of servers hold resources that a larger number of clients consume, and in which the failure or removal of a server disrupts every client depending on it.

The absence of a central point in a peer-to-peer network means there is no single target for censorship, denial of service, or legal compulsion, which is generally cited as the main source of resilience in this class of architecture.

Locating resources within a peer-to-peer network without a central index is commonly achieved using a distributed hash table, a class of algorithm that includes Kademlia, used in BitTorrent and I2P, and Chord. Peer-to-peer networks are described as structured when they use a distributed hash table to provide deterministic resource location, and as unstructured when they instead rely on flooding or random walks, an approach that is simpler to implement but less efficient at locating resources.

Peer-to-peer networks must also account for churn, the continuous joining and leaving of nodes, and resilient protocols are designed to maintain connectivity and data availability despite high rates of churn.

Open peer-to-peer systems without identity verification are exposed to Sybil attacks, in which an adversary creates a large number of fake identities to gain disproportionate influence over the network, and to eclipse attacks, in which an adversary surrounds a target node with malicious peers, controlling all of its connections and isolating it from the rest of the network.

Content addressing, the practice of identifying data by the cryptographic hash of its content rather than by its network location, is used in systems such as IPFS and BitTorrent. This approach ensures data integrity and allows retrieval of a given piece of content from any node that holds it, regardless of that node's location.

Purely altruistic resource sharing tends to produce free-rider problems, in which participants consume resources without contributing their own. Some peer-to-peer systems address this through explicit incentive mechanisms, such as BitTorrent's tit-for-tat algorithm for exchanging file segments, or the cryptocurrency-based rewards used in Filecoin.

Participating in a peer-to-peer network exposes a node's IP address to other participants by default. Overlay networks such as Tor and I2P add an anonymisation layer on top of this baseline, at the cost of additional latency introduced by routing traffic through intermediate relays.

Systems commonly cited as examples of these principles include BitTorrent for file distribution, I2P and Tor for anonymous communication, IPFS for content-addressed storage, Secure Scuttlebutt for offline-first social communication, and Meshtastic for LoRa-based mesh messaging.
