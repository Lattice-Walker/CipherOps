#  Quantum Telecommunications

## 1 Fundamentals of Quantum Physics Applied to Communications

![Qubit](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/9illustrations/9Qubit.svg)

A quantum bit, or qubit, is the basic unit of quantum information. Unlike a classical bit, which holds a value of either 0 or 1, a qubit can exist in a superposition of both states simultaneously until it is measured.

![Superposition](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/9illustrations/9Superposition.svg)

Superposition describes a quantum system existing in a combination of multiple states at once. Measurement collapses this superposition to a single definite outcome, with a probability determined by the system's state prior to measurement. This measurement process irreversibly disturbs the quantum state: the outcome is probabilistic, and the pre-measurement state cannot be recovered once collapse has occurred.

![Entanglement](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/9illustrations/9Entanglement.svg)

Entanglement is a correlation between two or more qubits such that the state of one is determined by the measurement of another, regardless of the physical distance separating them. This phenomenon was described by Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen in 1935 and was experimentally confirmed by Alain Aspect in 1982.

The no-cloning theorem establishes that it is impossible to create an identical copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. It was proved independently by Wootters and Zurek and by Dieks in 1982, and this property underlies much of quantum cryptography. A related constraint, the no-signalling principle, holds that entanglement cannot be used to transmit information faster than light: correlations between entangled particles only become apparent once measurement results are compared through a classical communication channel.

Quantum decoherence occurs when interaction between a quantum system and its environment causes it to lose its quantum properties and behave classically. Decoherence is the primary practical obstacle to maintaining qubits over time and over distance.

Photon polarisation, the orientation of the oscillation of a photon's electromagnetic field, can encode qubit values and is the most common physical carrier used in quantum communication systems. Quantum states can be measured in different bases, such as the rectilinear and diagonal bases used in polarisation encoding. Measuring a state in a basis other than the one in which it was prepared yields a random result and destroys the original state.

**Quantum Communication**

![Quantum Communication](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/9illustrations/9QuantumCommunication.svg)

A classical channel transmits bits that can be copied, amplified, and read without disturbing them. A quantum channel transmits qubits, and any interception of these qubits necessarily disturbs the transmitted state, a property exploited for security purposes in quantum key distribution.

Information in a quantum channel is encoded in physical properties of particles, most commonly the polarisation of single photons or the phase of coherent light pulses. Ideal quantum communication requires sources that emit exactly one photon at a time. Practical implementations instead commonly use attenuated laser pulses, which occasionally emit more than one photon, or sources based on spontaneous parametric down-conversion.

Optical fibre is the primary medium used for terrestrial quantum communication, while free-space optical links are used for satellite-based quantum communication. Photons are absorbed or scattered as they travel through fibre, and this loss increases exponentially with distance. As a result, current fibre-based quantum links are generally limited to a range of roughly 100 to 200 kilometres without the use of quantum repeaters.

Quantum repeaters are devices that would extend the range of quantum communication using entanglement swapping and quantum memory. As of the mid-2020s, quantum repeaters are not yet available at commercial scale. An interim approach to the distance limitation is the trusted node architecture, in which a network is divided into segments, each connected by a classical relay node. The security of this architecture depends on the trustworthiness of every relay node in the path, since each node has access to the key material in unencrypted form.

Quantum communication does not replace classical networks. It requires a parallel classical channel for functions including synchronisation, basis reconciliation, and error correction.

## 2 Quantum Key Distribution

![Quantum Key Distribution](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/networks/9illustrations/9QuantumKeyDistribution.svg)

Quantum Key Distribution is a method by which two parties establish a shared secret key using a quantum channel, with security derived from the laws of physics rather than from computational assumptions. This distinguishes it from schemes such as RSA and elliptic curve cryptography, whose security depends on the assumed difficulty of specific mathematical problems. The security of Quantum Key Distribution instead relies on the no-cloning theorem and on the disturbance that measurement necessarily causes to a quantum state.

The first Quantum Key Distribution protocol, BB84, was proposed by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984. In this protocol, one party, conventionally named Alice, encodes key bits in the polarisation of single photons using two conjugate bases. The other party, Bob, measures each photon in a randomly chosen basis. Alice and Bob then compare, over a classical channel, which basis was used for each photon, without revealing the bit value itself, and retain only the bits for which their bases matched. This process, known as basis sifting, produces what is referred to as the raw key.

Alice and Bob subsequently sacrifice a subset of the raw key to estimate the quantum bit error rate. A high error rate indicates either channel noise or the presence of an eavesdropper. A classical post-processing step called privacy amplification then compresses the raw key to remove any information a potential eavesdropper may have obtained, producing a shorter key that is provably secure under the protocol's assumptions.

An alternative protocol, E91, was proposed by Artur Ekert in 1991. It uses entangled photon pairs rather than individually prepared states, and verifies security through Bell inequality tests, which detect any eavesdropping that disturbs the correlations characteristic of entanglement.

Continuous-variable Quantum Key Distribution encodes key information in the continuous quadrature amplitudes of coherent light, rather than in discrete photon polarisation states. This approach is compatible with standard telecommunications components but requires a distinct security analysis from discrete-variable protocols such as BB84.

A key established through Quantum Key Distribution is described as information-theoretically secure: it is secure against an adversary with unlimited computational power, including a quantum computer, in contrast to the computational security offered by RSA and elliptic curve cryptography. Quantum Key Distribution is most naturally paired with the one-time pad cipher, which is information-theoretically secure when the key is as long as the message and used only once. Quantum Key Distribution provides a practical means of continuously generating and refreshing keys of the length required for this cipher.

### 2.1 Quantum Network Architecture

The simplest form of quantum network is a point-to-point link, in which a single quantum channel connects two nodes directly, limited by fibre loss to a range of roughly 100 to 200 kilometres.

A trusted node network extends this range by connecting multiple point-to-point links through classical relay nodes. Each segment establishes its own key, and the relay node decrypts and re-encrypts the key material as it passes through, meaning that the overall security of the network depends on the physical and operational security of every relay node.

A true quantum network, in which quantum states are routed without being measured, would require quantum switches and routers, which are not yet available at scale. An entanglement distribution network is a related architecture in which entangled photon pairs are distributed to distant nodes, enabling E91-style Quantum Key Distribution and, in principle, quantum teleportation. Such a network requires quantum memory, a device that stores a quantum state for a controlled period, in order to synchronise entanglement across multiple links. Current quantum memory implementations operate at cryogenic temperatures and have short coherence times.

Metropolitan quantum networks connecting multiple organisations over optical fibre have been deployed at city scale, with examples in Tokyo, Beijing, and Geneva. Satellite-based quantum communication extends this reach further: satellites can distribute entangled photons or relay Quantum Key Distribution over intercontinental distances. The Chinese Micius satellite demonstrated satellite-to-ground Quantum Key Distribution over 1,200 kilometres in 2017.

Quantum Key Distribution networks typically run alongside classical fibre networks, with the quantum channel generally occupying a dedicated wavelength or a dedicated fibre to avoid interference from classical signals. Research has nonetheless demonstrated coexistence of Quantum Key Distribution and classical data transmission on the same fibre using wavelength-division multiplexing, although Raman scattering generated by classical signals introduces additional noise on the quantum channel in this configuration.

### 2.2 Security and Eavesdropping Detection

Any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept and measure photons on a quantum channel introduces detectable errors, because measuring a quantum state in a basis other than the one in which it was prepared disturbs it irreversibly. This property forms the basis of eavesdropping detection in Quantum Key Distribution.

In an intercept-and-resend attack, an eavesdropper measures each photon and sends a newly prepared photon to Bob based on the measurement result. When applied against BB84, this attack introduces a 25 percent error rate in the sifted key, which Alice and Bob can detect during error rate estimation.

Quantum Key Distribution does not inherently authenticate the identity of the communicating parties. Without authentication, an attacker controlling the quantum channel could carry out a man-in-the-middle attack, impersonating both Alice and Bob. Preventing this requires authenticating the classical channel using a pre-shared secret or another classical authentication scheme.

Practical Quantum Key Distribution systems that use attenuated laser pulses occasionally emit more than one photon per pulse. In a photon number splitting attack, an eavesdropper splits off the additional photon and measures it later without disturbing the single-photon component of the signal. This attack is mitigated through decoy state protocols, which randomise the intensity of transmitted pulses to make such interception detectable.

Real single-photon detectors have physical imperfections that can be exploited through detector side-channel attacks. Blinding attacks, for example, use bright light to force a detector into a classical operating regime in which it can be controlled by the eavesdropper. Such an attack was demonstrated experimentally by Lydersen et al. in 2010.

Measurement-device-independent Quantum Key Distribution removes detector side channels by delegating the measurement step to an untrusted third party, so that security is preserved even if the measurement device itself is compromised. Device-independent Quantum Key Distribution goes further, basing security solely on observed violations of Bell inequalities, without any assumption about the internal operation of the devices used. This variant requires very high-efficiency detectors and remains largely experimental.

Modern security analysis of Quantum Key Distribution generally uses composable security frameworks, which guarantee that a generated key can be safely used in any subsequent cryptographic protocol rather than only under the specific conditions of its generation. In BB84, a quantum bit error rate below approximately 11 percent is generally considered consistent with the ability to extract a secure key after privacy amplification; above this threshold, the protocol is aborted.

### 2.3 Pilot Projects and Deployments

The DARPA Quantum Network, operated in the Boston area by BBN Technologies from 2004, was the first multi-node Quantum Key Distribution network. It connected six nodes over optical fibre and demonstrated continuous key generation.

The Tokyo QKD Network, established in 2010, is a metropolitan network connecting several research institutions and companies, including Toshiba, NEC, NTT, and Mitsubishi. It demonstrated Quantum Key Distribution-secured video conferencing.

The SECOQC network, deployed in Vienna in 2008, connected eight nodes across the city using multiple Quantum Key Distribution technologies and demonstrated interoperability between different implementations.

A Chinese national quantum backbone, built between 2017 and 2021, links Beijing and Shanghai over a 2,000-kilometre fibre connection using 32 trusted relay nodes. It has been used for government and financial communications and was extended by the Micius satellite to provide intercontinental reach.

The Micius satellite, launched in 2016, has demonstrated satellite-to-ground Quantum Key Distribution over 1,200 kilometres, entanglement distribution over the same distance, and quantum teleportation, with results published in Nature in 2017 and 2018.

Toshiba's Cambridge Research Laboratory has demonstrated continuous Quantum Key Distribution over installed metropolitan fibre, achieving key rates sufficient to support one-time pad encryption for low-bandwidth communications.

The European Quantum Internet Alliance is a coordinated research initiative working toward a pan-European quantum communication infrastructure. The related EuroQCI initiative aims to integrate quantum communication into European governmental networks. The OpenQKD project is a separate European testbed deploying Quantum Key Distribution infrastructure across multiple countries to evaluate its integration with classical networks and its performance in real-world applications.

Commercial Quantum Key Distribution products and services have been offered or trialled by vendors including ID Quantique in Switzerland, Toshiba in Japan and the United Kingdom, QuantumCTek in China, and Telefónica in Spain.

Deployed Quantum Key Distribution systems remain limited to metropolitan distances without the use of trusted nodes, generally operate at key rates in the range of kilobits per second, require either dedicated fibre or carefully managed wavelength allocation, and involve significant hardware costs. According to the sources describing these deployments, these factors continue to constrain widespread adoption.
