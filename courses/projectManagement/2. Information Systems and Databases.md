### Information Systems and Databases

An information system is an organized set of people, processes, data, and technology that collects, stores, processes, and distributes information to support decision-making and operations within an organization. This definition, commonly used in information systems literature, distinguishes an information system from the technology that supports it: the technology is one component among several, rather than the system itself.

An information system is generally described as composed of five interacting elements:

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Hardware | The physical devices used to input, process, store, and output data, such as servers, workstations, and network equipment |
| Software | The programs that direct the hardware to process data, including operating systems and application software |
| Data | The raw facts stored and processed by the system, which become information once organized in a way that is meaningful to a user |
| Procedures | The documented steps that people follow to use the system, such as instructions for entering an order or generating a report |
| People | The individuals who interact with the system, including end users, operators, and staff responsible for its maintenance |

Consider an order management system used by a retail company. Its hardware includes the servers hosting the application and the point-of-sale terminals in stores. Its software includes the order processing application and the database management system that stores transaction records. Its data includes customer records, product catalogs, and order histories. Its procedures include the sequence of steps a store employee follows to process a return. Its people include the store employees who enter transactions, the IT staff who maintain the servers, and the managers who consult sales reports generated by the system.

**Classification by organizational level**

Information systems are commonly classified according to the organizational level they support:

| Type | Organizational level | Example function |
|---|---|---|
| Transaction processing system | Operational | Recording individual sales transactions at a point-of-sale terminal |
| Management information system | Middle management | Producing periodic summary reports of sales by region |
| Decision support system | Middle to senior management | Modeling the effect of a proposed price change on projected revenue |
| Executive information system | Senior management | Presenting a consolidated view of company-wide performance indicators |

A transaction processing system typically generates the raw data that a management information system aggregates into reports, which in turn can feed into the models used by a decision support system. This layering illustrates how the same underlying data can serve different informational needs depending on the organizational level at which it is used.

**Alignment between information systems strategy and business strategy**

A recurring question in this area is whether an organization's information systems strategy is developed to support its business strategy, or whether the two are developed jointly. For example, if a retail company's business strategy is to compete on customer service, its information systems strategy might prioritize investment in a customer relationship management system that gives store employees access to a customer's purchase history, rather than investment in systems that primarily reduce internal processing costs.

**Competitive use of information systems**

Michael Porter and Victor Millar, in "How Information Gives You Competitive Advantage," published in the Harvard Business Review in 1985, describe how information systems can be analyzed using the value chain, a model that decomposes an organization's activities into primary activities, such as inbound logistics and operations, and support activities, such as procurement and technology development. According to this model, an information system can create competitive advantage by reducing the cost of an activity, by differentiating the organization's output, or by altering the scope of competition, for example by enabling a company to serve a market segment that competitors cannot reach cost-effectively.

**Make-or-buy decisions**

Organizations developing an information system face a decision between building a custom system internally, or through the contractor arrangement described in section 2.2, and purchasing a commercial off-the-shelf package. This decision typically involves a comparison between the two approaches:

| Factor | Custom development | Commercial package |
|---|---|---|
| Initial cost | Generally higher | Generally lower |
| Fit with existing business processes | Can be tailored precisely | May require adapting processes to the package |
| Time to deployment | Generally longer | Generally shorter |
| Long-term maintenance | Responsibility of the organization or its contractor | Often covered by vendor support agreements |
| Dependence on external vendor | Lower | Higher |

**Governance of information systems investment**

Decisions about which information systems to develop or acquire are typically made through a governance structure distinct from the project governance described in section 2.3, often involving an information systems steering committee composed of senior business and technology managers. This committee evaluates proposed information systems investments against criteria such as expected return on investment, alignment with business strategy, and available implementation capacity, and prioritizes among competing proposals accordingly.

## 1. Agile and Iterative Project Management Methods

### 1.1 Scrum

Scrum is an iterative framework for managing product development, organised around fixed-length iterations called sprints, typically lasting between one and four weeks.

![SCRUM](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/SCRUM.svg)

**Roles**
- Product Owner: responsible for defining and prioritising the product backlog.
- Scrum Master: responsible for facilitating the process and removing obstacles encountered by the team.
- Development Team: a cross-functional group responsible for delivering a usable increment at the end of each sprint.

**Ceremonies**
- Sprint Planning: the team selects backlog items to be completed during the upcoming sprint.
- Daily Scrum: a short daily meeting in which team members report progress and identify obstacles.
- Sprint Review: the team presents the completed work to stakeholders.
- Sprint Retrospective: the team examines its own process and identifies adjustments for subsequent sprints.

**Artefacts**
- Product Backlog: an ordered list of features, changes, and fixes required for the product.
- Sprint Backlog: the subset of backlog items selected for the current sprint, along with the plan for delivering them.
- Increment: the sum of completed backlog items at the end of a sprint, meeting the team's definition of done.

### 1.2 Kanban

Kanban is a method for managing work that emphasises continuous flow rather than fixed iterations. Work items are represented on a board, typically divided into columns corresponding to stages such as "to do," "in progress," and "done."

![Kanban](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/Kanban.svg)

Kanban relies on two main mechanisms:
- Work-in-progress limits, which cap the number of items permitted in a given stage at one time, intended to reduce bottlenecks.
- Visualisation of workflow, allowing team members to identify the status of tasks and locate points of delay.

Unlike Scrum, Kanban does not prescribe fixed roles, iteration lengths, or ceremonies. Teams can adopt it incrementally, applying it to existing processes without immediate structural change.

### 1.3 Extreme Programming

Extreme Programming is a software development methodology that specifies technical practices in addition to process guidelines. It was formalised by Kent Beck in the late 1990s.

![ExP](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/ExP.svg)

Core practices associated with Extreme Programming include:
- Pair programming, in which two developers work together at a single workstation.
- Test-driven development, in which automated tests are written before the corresponding code.
- Continuous integration, in which code changes are merged and tested frequently.
- Small, frequent releases, intended to shorten feedback cycles with users and stakeholders.
- Collective code ownership, in which any team member may modify any part of the codebase.

Extreme Programming shares Scrum's use of short iterations but places comparatively greater emphasis on engineering practices than on process roles or ceremonies.

### 1.4 Comparison of Methods

| Dimension | Scrum | Kanban | Extreme Programming |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | Fixed-length sprints | Continuous flow | Short iterations |
| Defined roles | Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team | None prescribed | Not strictly defined |
| Primary control mechanism | Sprint planning and review | Work-in-progress limits | Technical practices and testing |
| Focus | Process and product delivery | Workflow efficiency | Code quality and engineering discipline |
| Typical use case | Product development with evolving requirements | Operational or maintenance work with variable demand | Software projects requiring high technical quality |


## 2. Merise Modelling

Merise is a systems analysis and design method which separates the analysis of data from the analysis of processing, and structures system design into successive levels of abstraction:
- Conceptual level: describes what the system represents, independent of technical or organisational constraints.
- Logical (or organisational) level: describes how data and processing are organised, taking into account organisational choices such as task distribution.
- Physical level: describes how the system is implemented on a given technical platform.

Merise applies this three-level structure separately to data and to processing, producing distinct models for each dimension at each level.

### 2.1 Conceptual Data Model

The Conceptual Data Model represents the data of an information system independently of any technical or organisational implementation.

![CDM](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/CDM.svg)

Its principal components are:
- Entities: objects or concepts about which data is recorded, each defined by a set of attributes.
- Associations (or relationships): links between entities, which may themselves carry attributes.
- Cardinalities: constraints specifying the minimum and maximum number of times an entity instance can participate in a given association.

The conceptual data model is typically represented as an entity-relationship diagram and serves as the basis for subsequent stages of the design process.

### 2.2 Transition to the Logical Data Model

The logical data model is derived from the conceptual data model by applying a set of transformation rules. This stage introduces organisational and technological orientations, such as the choice of a relational, hierarchical, or network data structure, without yet specifying the details of a particular database management system.

![LDM](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/LDM.svg)

Common transformation rules include:
- Converting entities into tables (in the relational case), with attributes becoming columns.
- Converting one-to-many associations into foreign keys linking the corresponding tables.
- Converting many-to-many associations into separate association tables.

### 2.3 Physical Data Model

The physical data model specifies the implementation of the logical data model within a particular database management system. It includes details such as data types specific to the chosen system, indexing strategies, storage parameters, and integrity constraints implemented at the database level.

![PDM](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/PDM.svg)

Unlike the conceptual and logical models, the physical data model is dependent on the technical environment and can differ between implementations of the same logical model, depending on the database management system selected.


## 3. Use Case Diagrams

The Unified Modeling Language, developed in the 1990s through the work of Grady Booch, Ivar Jacobson, and James Rumbaugh, is a standardised notation for describing the structure and behaviour of software systems. It is maintained by the Object Management Group.

UML defines several diagram types, grouped into two broad categories:
- Structural diagrams, such as class diagrams and component diagrams, which represent the static organisation of a system.
- Behavioural diagrams, such as use case diagrams, sequence diagrams, and state machine diagrams, which represent the dynamic behaviour of a system.

Use case diagrams belong to the behavioural category and are primarily used during the requirements analysis phase of a project.

## 3.1 Use Case Design

A use case describes a sequence of interactions between a system and one or more external actors, aimed at achieving a specific goal. Each use case is represented in a diagram as an oval, labelled with a short verb phrase describing the goal (for example, "Withdraw Cash" or "Register Account").

![UCD](https://lattice-walker.github.io/CipherOps/courses/projectManagement/illustrations/UCD.svg)

- **An actor** represents a role played by a person, organisation, or external system that interacts with the system under study. Actors are classified as:
   - Primary actors, who initiate a use case to achieve a goal.
   - Secondary actors, who participate in a use case but do not initiate it, often providing a service or information requested by the system.
- **A scenario** is a specific sequence of actions and events representing one path through a use case, including the main success scenario and alternative scenarios corresponding to variations or exceptions.

Use case diagrams define several types of relationships:
- Association: a link between an actor and a use case, indicating participation.
- Include: indicates that one use case incorporates the behaviour of another use case as a required part of its execution.
- Extend: indicates that one use case may optionally incorporate the behaviour of another use case under specific conditions.
- Generalisation: indicates that one actor or use case inherits the behaviour and relationships of a more general actor or use case.

Use cases are commonly used as a basis for deriving functional requirements. Each use case can be traced to one or more functional specifications describing the expected behaviour of the system in response to actor interactions.

This traceability is typically documented through:
- Requirement tables that map individual use cases to corresponding functional requirements.
- Textual use case descriptions that serve as a starting point for drafting detailed specifications, including business rules and data validation criteria.
- Acceptance criteria derived from the main success scenario and alternative flows of each use case, used during testing to verify that implemented functionality matches the specified behaviour.
